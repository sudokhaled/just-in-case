> [!CAUTION]
> **Wet plus wind kills.** You can get deadly hypothermia when the air is above 4°C (40°F) if you are wet and in wind. Get out of the wind and get dry before anything else.
>
> **Never burn charcoal, a grill, a camp stove or a generator inside a tent, car or closed room to keep warm.** They make carbon monoxide, a gas that kills without warning. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).
>
> **Alcohol does not warm you.** It makes you feel warm while your body loses heat faster, and it ruins judgment.

## Quick steps

1. **Get out of the wind and rain.** Use a building, a car, a tarp, a rock, or the side of anything that blocks the wind.
2. **Get dry.** Take off wet clothes. Put on dry ones, or wrap yourself in a blanket, sleeping bag or plastic sheet.
3. **Get off the ground.** Sit and sleep on a thick layer: foam mat, cardboard, branches, dry leaves or a backpack.
4. **Cover your head, neck, hands and feet.** Use a hat, scarf, mittens and dry socks.
5. **Eat and drink.** Warm, sweet drinks and fatty food help your body make heat.
6. **Share warmth.** Huddle close to other people under shared covers.
7. **Watch for danger signs.** Strong shivering that will not stop, clumsiness, slurred speech or confusion means hypothermia. Act now (see the last section).

## How your body loses heat

| Way | What happens | How to stop it |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction | Touching cold things: ground, snow, stone, metal, wet clothes | Insulate under you, stay dry |
| Convection | Wind or moving water carries warm air away from your skin | Windproof outer layer, shelter |
| Radiation | Heat leaves any uncovered skin | Cover up, small shelter, reflective blanket |
| Evaporation | Sweat and wet skin cool you | Do not sweat, remove a layer before hard work |
| Breathing | Every cold breath must be warmed and moistened | Breathe through a scarf or cloth |

Water conducts heat about 25 times better than air. That is why wet clothes and wet ground are so dangerous.

## Dress in layers

Several thin layers are warmer than one thick layer. Still air trapped between layers holds heat. You can take off or add a layer to stay warm without sweating.

1. **Inner layer (next to skin):** wool or synthetic fabric (polyester, polypropylene). It moves sweat away from the skin.
2. **Middle layers (warmth):** fleece, wool sweater, down jacket or synthetic padded jacket. Add more middle layers as it gets colder.
3. **Outer layer (shell):** windproof and, if possible, waterproof. A rain jacket, a plastic poncho, or a large trash bag with holes for your head and arms.

The US Army teaches 4 rules for cold-weather clothing:

- **Keep it clean.** Dirt and grease flatten clothing so it holds less warm air.
- **Avoid overheating.** Open or remove layers before you start to sweat.
- **Wear it loose and in layers.** Tight clothes and tight boots squeeze out warm air and slow blood flow to hands and feet.
- **Keep it dry.** Wet clothing loses most of its warmth.

### Cotton kills when wet

Cotton (jeans, t-shirts, sweatshirts, cotton socks) soaks up water, dries slowly and stops keeping you warm when wet. In cold, wet weather, wear wool or synthetic fabric. If cotton is all you have, keep it under a waterproof layer and change it as soon as it gets damp.

Down (feathers) is very warm when dry but almost useless when wet. Wool keeps some warmth even when damp.

### Hands, feet and face

- Mittens are warmer than gloves. Spare socks work as mittens.
- Keep 1 pair of socks dry for sleeping. Change damp socks at least once a day. Dry damp socks and gloves inside your jacket, against your body.
- If boots feel tight, wear fewer socks. Tight boots cause frostbite.
- Wiggle your toes and fingers often.
- Cover your face in strong wind. Check each other's faces for white or gray patches, which are early frostbite.

## Stay dry

- **Do not sweat.** Before you walk, dig or carry, take off a layer and work slower. Put the layer back on as soon as you stop.
- Brush snow off your clothes before you enter a shelter. Snow melts and soaks in.
- Dry wet clothes near a fire, but not too close. Synthetic fabric melts and burns.
- Keep spare dry clothes in a sealed plastic bag.

## Insulate yourself from the ground

The ground pulls heat out of you faster than cold air does. Sitting or lying on snow, rock or bare earth is one of the fastest ways to get dangerously cold.

- Put **more insulation under you** than over you.
- Use a foam mat, cardboard, folded blankets, a backpack, car seat cushions, evergreen branches, or dry leaves, grass or straw.
- Pile loose material at least **30 cm (1 ft) deep**. It squashes flat when you lie on it. You should not feel the cold ground.
- Never sit directly on snow, ice, stone or metal.

## Improvised insulation

Anything that traps still, dry air keeps you warm.

- **Dry leaves, grass, pine needles, straw:** stuff them between 2 layers of clothing, or fill a sack, pillowcase or spare shirt to make a pad or quilt.
- **Newspaper and paper:** crumple sheets and stuff them inside your jacket or between blankets. Flat sheets under clothing also block wind.
- **Plastic bags and plastic sheets:** they block wind and rain, but they do not insulate. Wear a large trash bag over your clothes as a poncho, with a hole for your face. **Never put plastic over a child's head or face.**
- **Plastic bag as a vapor barrier:** in dry, freezing cold, wear a thin sock, then a plastic bag, then a thick sock. Sweat stays inside the bag, so the thick sock stays dry and warm. Dry your feet and change the thin sock every day.
- **Foil emergency blanket ("space blanket"):** it blocks wind and water and reflects some heat back. It is thin, so wrap it outside a blanket or your clothes, and still put insulation under you.

## Eat and drink

Your body burns food to make heat. Shivering burns energy fast.

- Eat more than usual. Fat (oil, butter, nuts, meat fat) gives long-lasting energy. Sugar and starchy food (bread, rice, sweets) give quick energy.
- Eat a snack before sleeping. Keep another to eat if you wake up cold.
- Drink even if you are not thirsty. Cold air is dry, and you lose a lot of water by breathing and urinating. Warm drinks help a little and lift morale.
- **Do not eat snow.** Melt it first, and treat it like any other water. Eating snow cools your body.

## Share body heat

- Huddle together, with shared blankets over the group and insulation under it.
- Put children and the coldest people in the middle.
- 2 people under the same covers stay warmer than 2 people apart.

## Hot water bottles and warm stones

1. Fill a strong container with hot water: a metal bottle, a rubber hot water bottle, or a hard plastic bottle made for hot drinks. Thin drink bottles can soften, leak or burst. Use hot, not boiling, water in rubber and plastic.
2. Close the lid tightly and check that it does not leak.
3. Wrap it in a sock or cloth.
4. Hold it against your chest, in your armpits or between your thighs.

You can also heat dry stones near a fire and wrap them in cloth. **Never heat stones taken from rivers or wet ground.** Water trapped inside can make them crack or burst.

> [!WARNING]
> People who are very cold, asleep, elderly, numb, or have diabetes may not feel a burn. Check the skin often. Do not give hot water bottles to babies. Never put heat directly on frostbitten skin.

## Sleeping warm

1. Change into dry clothes before bed. Keep 1 dry set only for sleeping.
2. Put at least as much insulation under you as over you.
3. Shake your sleeping bag or blankets to fluff them up. Squashed insulation does not work.
4. Wear a hat. Close the hood or tuck a blanket around your neck so warm air cannot escape.
5. Eat and drink something before sleeping. If you wake up cold, eat again, and tense and relax your muscles for a few minutes.
6. Breathe outside the sleeping bag. The moisture in your breath makes it damp.
7. In freezing weather, keep your water bottle and tomorrow's dry socks inside the sleeping bag so they do not freeze.

A small shelter warms up faster than a big one. See [Building an Emergency Shelter](../shelter/emergency-shelter.md). At home without power, see [Keeping a Home Warm Without Power](../shelter/heat-home-no-power.md). A fire outdoors, with a wall of logs or rocks behind it to reflect heat, helps a lot: see [Making and Keeping a Fire](../fire/make-fire.md).

## Early signs of hypothermia

Check yourself and each other often. A cold person often does not notice their own confusion.

| Stage | Signs |
|---|---|
| Mild | Shivering, cold pale skin, numb hands, clumsy fingers, tiredness |
| Getting worse | Stumbling, slurred speech, fumbling, bad mood, poor decisions |
| Severe | Shivering stops while still cold, confusion, very sleepy, unconscious |

If you see these signs, act at once:

1. Get the person out of wind and wet, and put insulation under them.
2. Replace wet clothes with dry ones, or cover them with dry layers and a waterproof or foil outer layer.
3. If they are fully awake and can swallow, give warm, sweet drinks and food.
4. Handle a severely cold person gently and keep them lying flat.

Then follow [Hypothermia and Frostbite](../medical/hypothermia-frostbite.md). Frostbite looks like white, gray or waxy skin that feels hard or numb. Do not rub it.

## Common mistakes and myths

- **"Alcohol warms you up."** Wrong. It speeds heat loss.
- **"Rub frozen skin with snow."** Wrong. It damages the skin more.
- **"Most heat leaves through the head."** Not exactly. The head loses about as much as any other uncovered skin of the same size. It is often the only uncovered part, so wear a hat.
- **"Hard exercise keeps you warm."** Only while you move. Sweat soaks your clothes, and you get colder when you stop.
