> [!CAUTION]
> **Confusion, strange behavior or collapse in the heat means heat stroke.** It can kill within hours. Cool the person **immediately** with cold water over as much of the body as possible. Do this before anything else. See [Heat Exhaustion and Heat Stroke](../medical/heat-illness.md).
>
> **Never leave a child, an older person or an animal in a parked car,** not even for a few minutes with the windows open. The inside heats up far above the outside temperature.
>
> **Do not ration water in the heat.** Drink the water you have. Save sweat instead: rest in shade and move only when it is cool.

## Quick steps

1. **Get out of the sun.** Stay in deep shade or the lowest, darkest room you can find.
2. **Wet your skin.** Sponge or splash water on your skin, or wear a wet cloth, and let moving air blow over it.
3. **Drink regularly,** before you feel thirsty. When working in heat, drink about 250 mL (1 cup) every 15 to 20 minutes.
4. **Do not do heavy work in the hottest hours,** usually late morning to late afternoon. Work early in the morning or after sunset.
5. **Check on others twice a day:** babies, older people, sick people and anyone who lives alone.
6. **Act on warning signs.** Dizziness, headache, nausea or heavy sweating: stop, rest in shade, cool down and drink. Confusion: cool the person now.

## Who is most at risk

Heat waves kill more people than most other weather disasters. In the summer of 2003, more than 70,000 extra people died in Europe, many of them older people alone at home.

- People over 65, especially those who live alone. Older people often do not feel thirsty or hot, even when they are in danger.
- Babies and children under 5.
- Pregnant women.
- People with heart, lung or kidney disease, diabetes, obesity or mental illness.
- People taking some medicines, such as diuretics ("water pills"), some heart and blood pressure medicines, and some medicines for mental illness. Do not stop a medicine on your own. Keep cool and drink regularly.
- People who cannot move or care for themselves. See [Elderly, Disabled and Chronically Ill People](../community/vulnerable-people.md).
- Outdoor workers, athletes, and anyone not used to the heat. The body takes about 1 to 2 weeks to adapt.

## Keep your home cool without air conditioning

### Block the sun

- Shade windows from the **outside** if you can: shutters, awnings, or a sheet, blanket, mat or cardboard hung outside the glass. Outside shades stop much more heat than inside curtains.
- If you can only shade from inside, use light-colored curtains, or cover the glass with foil or white cloth facing out. Dark curtains inside absorb heat.
- Keep curtains closed on any window that gets direct sun.

### Open at night, close in the day

- When it is cooler outside than inside (usually at night and early morning), open windows on opposite sides of the building so air flows through.
- When it becomes hotter outside than inside (usually by mid-morning), close windows and shades to keep the cool air in.
- If you have a thermometer, aim to keep rooms below 32°C (90°F) in the day and below 24°C (75°F) at night, especially for older people, babies and sick people.

### Use the coolest place

- Heat rises. Live and sleep in the lowest room: the ground floor, or a basement or cellar if it is safe and dry. Top floors and rooms under metal roofs are the most dangerous.
- Rooms facing away from the sun stay cooler: the north side in the northern half of the world, the south side in the southern half.
- Do not cook indoors in the heat of the day. Turn off lights and devices that make heat.
- If your home stays very hot, spend the hottest hours somewhere cooler: a cool public building, a shaded basement, or deep shade under trees.

## Cool your body

Water evaporating from your skin is the strongest cooling you have without electricity.

- **Wet skin and moving air:** sponge or spray water on your skin, or wear a wet shirt, scarf or hat. Fan yourself with a piece of cardboard, or sit in a breeze. Wet it again when it dries.
- **Cool water:** take a cool (not ice cold) shower, bath or bucket wash several times a day. Soak your feet and hands in cool water.
- **Wet sheet:** hang a wet sheet in an open window or doorway with a breeze. This works best in dry climates. In very humid air, wet cloths cool much less.
- **Clothes:** wear loose, thin, light-colored clothing, such as cotton or linen. Wear a wide hat outdoors.
- **Save water:** water for wetting skin does not need to be drinking water, but it must not be sewage or chemical water. Keep it out of your mouth, eyes and wounds.

> [!WARNING]
> **An electric fan alone may not help when the air is above about 35°C (95°F).** In that heat, a fan blows hot air on you, makes you sweat more, and can dehydrate you faster. **Wet your skin first, then use the fan.** For older people and sick people, the danger may start at lower temperatures. If you can, move somewhere cooler.

## Work and rest

- Do heavy work early in the morning or in the evening. Rest in shade during the hottest part of the day.
- Take frequent breaks in the shade. The hotter it is, the shorter the work periods and the longer the rests.
- If you are not used to heat, start slowly. On day 1, work in the heat for no more than 20% of your normal time. Add no more than 20% each day after that.
- Work in pairs and watch each other for warning signs.
- Stop at once if you feel dizzy, sick, confused or weak.

## Drinking

- **Do not wait for thirst.** Sip regularly through the day.
- When working in heat, drink about **250 mL (1 cup) every 15 to 20 minutes.**
- Check your urine. Pale yellow is good. Dark yellow, or very little urine, means drink more.
- **Do not drink more than about 1.4 liters (1.5 quarts) per hour, or about 11 liters (12 quarts) per day.** Too much plain water can dilute the salt in your blood, which is dangerous.
- **Replace salt** when you sweat for hours. Eat normal meals with salty food, or drink oral rehydration solution (ORS): 1 liter (1 quart) of safe water with 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt. See [Dehydration, Diarrhea and Oral Rehydration](../medical/dehydration.md).
- Avoid alcohol. It makes you lose water.
- Babies under 6 months who are breastfed do not need extra water. Breastfeed more often.
- If water is short, eat less, and avoid salty, dry and high-protein food. Digestion uses water.

## In the desert

These rules come from US Army survival training.

- **Move only at night, or in the cool early morning and evening.** Rest in shade during the day.
- **Keep your clothes on.** Cover your skin, head and neck with loose, light-colored clothes. Clothes slow sweat loss and prevent sunburn. Taking clothes off makes you lose water faster.
- **Ration sweat, not water.** Drink the water you have when you need it. Save body water by resting, moving slowly and staying in shade.
- **Get off the hot ground.** The ground surface can be much hotter than the air. Rest on something raised, or scrape away the hot top layer of sand.
- **Make double shade.** Two layers of cloth with an air gap between them are much cooler than one layer.
- **Stay with your vehicle** if it breaks down. It is easier for rescuers to see than a person. Rest in its shade, not inside it in the day.
- **Never drink urine, seawater, blood or alcohol.** They make dehydration worse.

To find water, see [Finding Water](../water/find-water.md). To live in a desert for weeks or longer, see [Living in the Desert](../land/desert-living.md).

## Warning signs

| Signs | Likely problem | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, muscle cramps | Heat exhaustion | Stop. Move to shade. Wet the skin and fan. Drink water or ORS. |
| Confusion, strange behavior, collapse, seizure, very hot skin (wet or dry) | Heat stroke: a deadly emergency | Cool immediately: put the person in cold water up to the neck, or soak the whole body and fan hard. Get help. |

Full treatment is in [Heat Exhaustion and Heat Stroke](../medical/heat-illness.md).

## Common mistakes and myths

- **"A fan always cools you."** Not in extreme heat. Wet the skin as well.
- **"Only old people are at risk."** Healthy young workers and athletes also die of heat stroke.
- **"Take off your shirt in the desert to stay cool."** Wrong. You lose more water and get sunburned.
- **"If they are still sweating, it cannot be heat stroke."** Wrong. Many people with heat stroke are still sweating. Confusion is the key sign.
- **"Save your water for later."** People have died with water left in their bottles. Drink it, and save sweat instead.
