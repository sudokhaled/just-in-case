> [!CAUTION]
> **Never shelter under dead trees or dead branches.** They fall without warning, especially in wind or snow. Look up before you build.
>
> **Never shelter in a dry riverbed, gully or narrow valley floor.** Rain far away can send a flash flood through in minutes.
>
> **Never use a stove, candle, fire, charcoal or running engine in a closed shelter, snow shelter, tent or car without fresh air.** Carbon monoxide kills without warning. In a car stuck in snow, keep the exhaust pipe clear. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).

## Quick steps

1. **Start early.** If you will be outside after dark in cold, wet or windy weather, start your shelter while there are still at least 2 hours of daylight.
2. **Use what is already there:** a car, a sound building, a rock overhang, a fallen tree, or the low branches of a thick evergreen tree.
3. **Pick a safe spot:** flat, dry, out of the wind, away from dead trees, water channels and steep slopes.
4. **Build the floor first:** dry leaves, grass or branches at least 30 cm (1 ft) deep.
5. **Keep it small:** just big enough to lie down in. Small shelters stay warmer.
6. **Make a roof that sheds rain and blocks wind,** with the entrance away from the wind.
7. **Make it easy to find:** put bright cloth or signals nearby. See [Signaling for Rescue](../navigation/signal-rescue.md).

## Choose a safe site

Look up, look around, look down.

| Danger | Why | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Dead trees and hanging branches ("widowmakers") | They fall without warning | Check every tree that could reach you if it fell |
| Dry riverbeds, gullies, low ground beside rivers | Flash floods, even when it is not raining where you are | Camp well above flood marks: lines of mud or debris on trees and rocks |
| Valley bottoms and hollows | Cold air sinks there at night | Camp part way up a slope |
| Hilltops and open ridges | Strong wind, lightning | Camp below the top, on the side away from the wind |
| Below cliffs and steep slopes | Rockfall, landslides, avalanches | Look for fallen rocks and bare tracks down the slope |
| Animal paths, ant and wasp nests | Bites, stings, animals at night | Move away |

A good site is flat and dry, has a natural wind block (rocks, thick bushes, a fallen log), and is close to building material. Water nearby is useful, but stay higher than the water and about 60 m (200 ft) away from it. To turn a camp into a home for months, see [Choosing Where to Live](../land/choosing-a-site.md) and [Building a Lasting Home From Natural Materials](../land/build-home.md).

## Size and insulation

- Build only big enough to lie down and turn over. Your body cannot heat a big space.
- Make the floor first. Pile dry leaves, grass, pine needles or evergreen branches at least **30 cm (1 ft)** deep. Lie on it to test it: you should not feel the cold ground.
- Thick walls of leaves, snow or soil keep heat in. A thin tarp or plastic sheet only stops wind and rain.
- See [Staying Warm in the Cold](../shelter/stay-warm.md) for clothing and sleeping warm.

## Tarp shelters

Any waterproof sheet works: a tarp, a plastic sheet, a shower curtain, or large trash bags cut open and taped together. To tie a sheet with no holes in it, wrap a small stone in the corner and tie the cord tightly around the bulge. For tying cord, see [Essential Knots](../skills/knots.md).

### A-frame (best for rain and wind)

1. Tie a rope tightly between 2 trees, about 1 m (3 ft) above the ground.
2. Hang the sheet over the rope so it falls equally on both sides.
3. Pull both edges out at a steep angle and hold them down with stakes, rocks or logs. Steep sides shed rain. Flat sheets collect water and sag.
4. Close the end facing the wind with a pack, branches or a folded corner of the sheet.

### Lean-to (good with a fire)

1. Tie a rope between 2 trees, about 1 to 1.2 m (3 to 4 ft) above the ground.
2. Tie one edge of the sheet along the rope. Pull the other edge down to the ground and hold it down.
3. The sloped back must face the wind. The open side faces away from the wind.
4. Build a fire about 1 m (3 ft) in front of the open side. Build a wall of logs or rocks behind the fire to reflect heat into the shelter. Keep sparks and flames away from plastic: it melts and burns.

A lean-to gives poor protection if the wind changes direction. Close the sides with branches.

## Debris hut (no tarp needed)

This is the warmest shelter you can build from forest material. It works like a sleeping bag made of leaves. It takes several hours and a very large pile of dry leaves.

1. Find a strong, straight ridgepole about 1 m (3 ft) longer than your height.
2. Rest one end on a stump, a rock or a forked stick, about 60 to 90 cm (2 to 3 ft) high. Rest the other end on the ground.
3. Lean sticks ("ribs") close together along both sides of the ridgepole. The inside should be just wide enough for your body. Leave an entrance at the high end.
4. Lay small branches and twigs across the ribs so leaves do not fall through.
5. Pile dry leaves, grass or pine needles over the frame until the layer is **at least 1 m (3 ft) thick.** Thicker is warmer.
6. Lay a few branches on top so wind does not blow the leaves away.
7. Fill the inside with a bed of dry leaves at least **30 cm (1 ft) deep.**
8. Crawl in feet first. Pull a pile of leaves, or a bag stuffed with leaves, into the entrance to close it.

## Snow shelters

Snow is a good insulator. Inside a well-built snow shelter, the air usually stays near 0°C (32°F), even when it is much colder outside.

Rules for every snow shelter:

- **Work slowly.** Digging makes you sweat. Remove layers while digging and keep dry clothes for afterwards.
- **Make a ventilation hole** in the roof, about 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) wide. Keep a stick in it and push it clear often, especially after snowfall.
- **Make the entrance lower than the sleeping area.** Cold air sinks and flows out. Warm air stays where you sleep.
- **Smooth the ceiling** into a dome so melting snow runs down the walls instead of dripping on you.
- **Keep a shovel, pot or plate inside** in case snow blocks the entrance.
- **Mark the outside** with a bright object so rescuers can find you.
- **Never cook or burn anything** unless the vent and entrance are open. A candle that burns badly can mean bad air, but carbon monoxide can kill before you notice anything. Do not rely on it.

### Snow trench (fastest)

1. Dig a trench as long as your body, about 1 m (3 ft) deep and just wide enough to lie in.
2. Lay branches, skis, poles or a tarp across the top. Cover them with snow blocks or loose snow.
3. Close one end. Make the entrance at the other end.
4. Line the floor with branches, a mat or a pack.

### Quinzee (hollowed snow pile)

1. Pile snow into a dome about 2 m (6 ft) high and 2.5 to 3 m (8 to 10 ft) wide. Mix and pat the snow as you pile it.
2. Push 20 to 30 sticks, each 30 cm (1 ft) long, into the dome all over. They show you the wall thickness when you dig.
3. **Wait at least 1 to 2 hours** for the snow to settle and harden. Wait longer if the snow is dry and powdery.
4. Dig a low entrance on the side away from the wind. Hollow out the inside. Stop digging when you reach the end of a stick.
5. Leave a raised sleeping platform higher than the entrance.
6. Push a stick through the roof to make the ventilation hole.

**Never dig a snow shelter alone if you can avoid it.** Have a partner wait outside in case the roof falls in.

## Using a car

If you are stuck in snow:

1. **Stay with the car.** It is a shelter, and rescuers find a car more easily than a person. Do not walk away unless you can see help less than about 100 m (100 yards) away. People get lost in blowing snow.
2. **Clear the exhaust pipe** of snow, and keep it clear. A blocked exhaust fills the car with carbon monoxide.
3. **Run the engine and heater for about 10 minutes each hour.** Open a window on the side away from the wind slightly while it runs.
4. Turn on the inside light while the engine runs, so rescuers can see the car at night. Tie bright cloth to the antenna or door.
5. Insulate yourself with floor mats, seat covers, newspaper, maps and spare clothes. Huddle together.
6. Move your arms and legs often to keep blood flowing.
7. Take turns sleeping, so one person watches for rescuers and checks the exhaust.

An electric car has no exhaust, but heating drains the battery. Heat in short bursts and use seat heaters, which use less power.

In hot desert, stay with the car too, but rest in its shade, not inside it, during the day.

## In a town or city

After an earthquake, explosion, storm or fire, damaged buildings can collapse, even hours or days later. Aftershocks bring down weakened buildings.

**Do not go into a building** if you see any of these:

- Large cracks in walls, foundations or chimneys, or walls that lean or bulge.
- A sagging roof or floor, or a building that has moved off its foundation.
- Doors and windows that suddenly jam, because the frame is twisted.
- A smell of gas or a hissing sound, broken electric wires, or flooding.

If you are not sure, stay outside. Shelter in an open area, far enough from buildings, walls, power lines and trees that falling material cannot reach you. Use what is around you: tarps, plastic, doors, cardboard for ground insulation, cars and bus shelters. If you smell gas, leave at once, and do not light flames or use switches.
