> [!CAUTION]
> **Do not share prescription medicines** unless a life is at immediate risk and there is no other choice. The wrong medicine or dose can kill.
>
> **Check the active ingredient on every package.** Many cold, flu and pain products contain paracetamol (acetaminophen). Taking two of them together is a common cause of deadly overdose.
>
> **Keep all medicines out of children's reach.** Iron tablets and adult medicines can kill a small child.

## Quick steps

1. **Gather the basics** in a waterproof box or bag: gloves, gauze and bandages, tape, a tourniquet, soap, oral rehydration salts, pain and fever medicine, a thermometer, and your family's daily medicines.
2. **Keep 1-3 months** of every essential prescription.
3. **Write a medicine card** for each family member.
4. **Store medicines cool, dry and dark,** in their original packages.
5. **Check the kit every 6 months.** Replace used and short-life items.

## Family kit checklist

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| Disposable gloves (nitrile), 10 pairs or more | Protect you and the patient from infection |
| Soap and hand sanitizer (60% alcohol or more) | Clean hands before and after care |
| Sterile gauze pads, several sizes | Cover wounds, pack bleeding wounds |
| Roller bandages and 2 elastic bandages | Hold dressings, support sprains, pressure bandage for some snakebites |
| Adhesive bandages and adhesive tape | Small cuts, holding dressings |
| 2 triangular bandages | Slings, padding, tying splints |
| Commercial tourniquet | Stop life-threatening bleeding from a limb |
| Fine-pointed tweezers | Splinters, ticks, stingers |
| Strong blunt-tipped scissors | Cut clothing, tape and bandages |
| Safety pins | Slings, bandages |
| Digital thermometer | Measure fever |
| Antiseptic (povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine) | Skin around wounds, animal bites |
| Plain petroleum jelly | Keeps small wounds moist, protects cracked skin |
| 10-20 mL syringe, no needle | Rinse wounds and eyes, measure liquid medicine |
| Oral rehydration salts, 10 packets or more | Diarrhea, vomiting, heat illness |
| Zinc tablets (if you have children) | Diarrhea in children |
| Paracetamol (acetaminophen), adult and child forms | Pain and fever |

Add insect repellent, sunscreen, and any medicines your family uses often.

## Improvised supplies

- **Dressings:** sanitary pads, disposable diapers, or clean cloth. To clean cloth, boil it for 10 minutes, then dry it in the sun or iron it hot.
- **Bandages:** strips torn from a clean t-shirt or sheet.
- **Gloves:** plastic bags over the hands.
- **Burn cover:** plastic wrap laid loosely over a cooled burn, or a clean plastic bag over a burned hand. Do not wrap it tightly around a limb. See [Burns](../medical/burns.md).
- **Tape:** duct tape holds splints and dressings, and prevents blisters on sore spots on the feet.
- **Splint:** rolled newspapers or magazines, sticks, cardboard or a pillow, padded with cloth.
- **Sling:** pin the front hem of a shirt up over the injured arm with safety pins.
- **Wound rinse:** clean drinking water squeezed from a plastic bag with a pinhole, or a bottle with a hole in the cap. Clean drinking water works as well as saline for most wounds.

## Storing medicines

- Keep them **below 25°C (77°F)** if you can, dry and out of light. Not in a bathroom, near a stove, in a car or in sun.
- Keep them in **original packages,** with the label, leaflet and expiry date. In humid places, use an airtight box.
- Store them high up and locked if possible.
- **Refrigerated medicines** such as insulin: keep at **2-8°C (36-46°F)** and **never freeze.** Most insulin in use lasts about 4 weeks at room temperature; check the leaflet. Without a fridge, keep it in the coolest shaded place, such as a clay pot cooled by evaporation. Throw away insulin that has frozen or has clumps.

## Expired medicines

The expiry date is the last date the maker guarantees full strength with correct storage. It is not the date the medicine becomes poison.

The US military's Shelf Life Extension Program tested stored medicines for decades. It found that many **solid medicines (tablets and capsules) kept their strength for years** past the printed date. Those medicines were kept in ideal conditions in sealed packages. Medicines kept at home may be weaker.

In a real emergency with nothing else, an expired tablet or capsule that looks normal is usually better than nothing.

**Do not use** any medicine that has changed color or smell, is crumbling or sticky, or is a liquid that has become cloudy or has particles.

**Replace these on time:**

- **Insulin.**
- **Adrenaline (epinephrine) auto-injectors.** But in a severe allergic reaction, use an expired one if it is the only one. It still contains some medicine.
- **Liquid antibiotics** that have been mixed with water, usually after 7-14 days. Check the label.
- **Nitroglycerin** (glyceryl trinitrate) tablets for chest pain, which lose strength quickly once opened.
- **Eye drops,** usually 4 weeks after opening.
- Medicines where small changes in strength matter: seizure medicines, thyroid medicines and blood thinners.
- **Tetracycline-family antibiotics.** Old reports linked broken-down tetracycline to kidney damage. Modern products are thought to be safer, but do not use expired ones.

## Keeping a prescription supply

- Keep **1-3 months** of essential medicines: for heart disease, blood pressure, diabetes, epilepsy, asthma, thyroid, mental illness, HIV, tuberculosis and contraception. See [Family Planning and Contraception](../medical/contraception.md).
- Ask your doctor or pharmacist for an emergency supply. Refill as soon as allowed, not when you run out. Use the oldest first.
- Carry at least a few days' supply in your emergency bag. See [Emergency Kit Checklists](../reference/emergency-kits.md).
- **Ask now which medicines are dangerous to stop suddenly,** such as steroids, seizure medicines, some heart and mental health medicines, and insulin for type 1 diabetes. Ask how to stretch your supply safely if you must. See [Withdrawal and Running Out of Medicines](../medical/withdrawal.md).

## Medicine card

For each family member, write on paper and keep it in a plastic bag:

- Name, date of birth and weight.
- Allergies, especially to medicines.
- Health conditions.
- Each medicine: generic name, strength, dose, times, and what it is for.
- Date of last tetanus vaccine.
- Blood group, if known.
- Doctor and emergency contacts.

## Reading labels

- **Find the active ingredient.** Brand names differ between countries. The generic name does not.
- **Check the strength:** mg per tablet, or mg per 5 mL for liquids. Children's liquids come in different strengths.
- **Check the dose,** how often, the maximum in 24 hours, age limits and warnings.
- **Measure liquids with a syringe or medicine cup,** not a kitchen spoon.

## Sharing medicines

Prescription medicines are chosen for one person. Sharing risks allergies, dangerous mixes and wrong doses. The exceptions are a life in immediate danger with no other option:

- An adrenaline auto-injector for anaphylaxis. See [Severe Allergic Reactions (Anaphylaxis)](../medical/allergic-reactions.md).
- A reliever inhaler for a severe asthma attack.
- Naloxone for an opioid overdose.

## Fake medicines

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that **1 in 10 medical products in low- and middle-income countries is substandard or falsified.** Fakes are most common from street markets, unlicensed sellers and websites, and during shortages.

Warning signs:

- Spelling mistakes, poor printing, or a missing batch number or expiry date.
- A different color, shape, size or taste from usual.
- Broken seals, a very low price, or tablets sold loose.

Buy from licensed pharmacies and health services. Keep the package. If a medicine does not work as usual or causes strange effects, stop and tell a health worker.

## When to get help

- **Emergency:** swelling of the face or throat, trouble breathing or a spreading rash after a medicine. Use adrenaline if available.
- Someone took too much medicine, or the wrong one. See [Poisoning](../medical/poisoning.md).
- You are running out of a medicine that is dangerous to stop.
- You are unsure of a dose, especially for a child, a pregnant woman or an old person.
