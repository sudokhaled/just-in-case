> [!CAUTION]
> **Give adrenaline (epinephrine) at once.** Do not wait to see if it gets worse. Delay is the main cause of death in anaphylaxis. If in doubt, inject.
>
> **Do not let the person stand up or walk,** even if they feel better. Standing can cause a sudden, fatal collapse.
>
> **Antihistamine tablets do not treat anaphylaxis.** They only help itching and rash. They never replace adrenaline.

## Quick steps

1. **Inject adrenaline** with an auto-injector into the outer thigh, through clothing if needed.
2. **Call emergency services.** Say "anaphylaxis".
3. **Lay them down** with legs raised. If breathing is hard, let them sit up with legs out straight. If pregnant, lay them on the left side.
4. **No improvement after 5 minutes?** Give a second dose of adrenaline, in the other thigh if possible.
5. **Not breathing normally?** Start CPR. See [CPR: When Someone Is Not Breathing](../medical/cpr.md).
6. **Stay with them** and watch them for at least 12-24 hours. Symptoms can come back.

## Recognizing anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis comes on **suddenly,** usually within minutes, and sometimes up to 2 hours after contact with the cause. Common causes are foods (peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, eggs, sesame), insect stings, medicines (some antibiotics and pain medicines), and latex.

Suspect anaphylaxis when someone suddenly has **breathing or circulation problems,** often with skin changes:

- **Airway:** swollen tongue, lips or throat, hoarse voice, trouble swallowing, a high-pitched noise when breathing in.
- **Breathing:** wheezing, shortness of breath, a cough that will not stop, blue or grey lips.
- **Circulation:** pale and clammy skin, dizziness, fainting, a fast weak pulse, collapse.
- **Skin:** hives (raised, itchy red welts), flushing, swelling of the face or eyes.
- **Other:** vomiting and belly cramps, a sense that something terrible is happening.

Skin signs are missing in some people. Breathing or circulation problems alone, after a known trigger, are enough to give adrenaline.

A rash or mild swelling **without** breathing trouble, dizziness or vomiting is usually a mild reaction. Watch closely, because it can turn severe.

## Using an adrenaline auto-injector

Devices differ. The instructions are printed on the side. The basic steps are the same:

1. **Take off the safety cap.** Hold the injector in your fist. Do not put your thumb over either end.
2. **Press the tip firmly into the outer middle of the thigh,** at a right angle. It works through trousers or a skirt.
3. **Hold it in place** for as long as the label says, often 3 to 10 seconds.
4. **Remove it** and rub the spot for a few seconds. Note the time.
5. Keep the used injector to show health workers.

The person may feel shaky, anxious, pale, or feel their heart pounding. These effects are normal and pass in minutes. They are far less dangerous than anaphylaxis.

- **Second dose:** if there is no improvement after **5 minutes,** give another auto-injector. Use the other thigh if possible.
- **Expired injector:** if it is the only one, use it. An expired injector still contains some adrenaline and is much better than none.
- **Child-sized injectors** contain a smaller dose for small children. Use the one prescribed for that person.
- In some countries, adrenaline also comes as a **nasal spray.** Use it as the package says.

> [!NOTE]
> Adrenaline from a vial or ampoule needs a syringe and a correct dose. Only someone trained should give it this way.

## Position

| Situation | Position |
|---|---|
| Faint, dizzy or pale | Lie flat with legs raised |
| Hard to breathe | Sit up with legs out straight |
| Unconscious but breathing | Recovery position, on the side |
| Pregnant | Lie on the left side |
| Not breathing normally | Flat on the back: start CPR |

**Never let them stand or walk,** even to get to a car or a bathroom. Carry them if they must be moved. If they feel better, let them sit up slowly.

For the recovery position, see [First Aid Basics: Check, Call, Care](../medical/first-aid-basics.md).

## Other things that help, after adrenaline

- **Remove the cause** if you can. Scrape out a bee sting with a fingernail or card. Stop any medicine that may have caused it.
- **Asthma inhaler:** if they wheeze and have an inhaler (salbutamol), give puffs as their instructions say.
- **Antihistamine tablets:** may help itching and hives once the person can swallow safely. Use the package dose.

## Watching afterwards

Anaphylaxis can come back hours later without any new contact. This is called a biphasic reaction. It usually happens within 12 hours.

- Everyone who had anaphylaxis should be seen in a hospital, even if they got better after adrenaline.
- **When no hospital can be reached,** stay with the person for **at least 24 hours.** Keep a second injector ready. Keep them resting.
- Give adrenaline again at once if symptoms return.

## When there is no adrenaline

There is no real substitute for adrenaline. Without it:

1. Lie them down as described above.
2. Remove the cause.
3. Give their asthma inhaler for wheezing, and antihistamine for itching and swelling.
4. Be ready to start CPR.
5. Evacuate urgently.

Many mild reactions get better on their own. Severe ones can kill within minutes.

## Evacuate if

- They needed adrenaline, even if they recovered.
- Symptoms come back.
- Breathing is still difficult, or they are still dizzy or pale after 2 doses.
- They collapsed or needed CPR.

## Preventing reactions

- **Know the trigger** and avoid it. Read food labels. Ask how food was prepared.
- People with known severe allergies should carry **2 adrenaline auto-injectors** at all times.
- Check the expiry date. Keep injectors at room temperature, never frozen or in a hot car.
- Teach family and friends where the injector is and how to use it.
- Wear a medical bracelet or carry a note that names the allergy.
- **Before a disaster:** keep spare injectors in your emergency kit. See [Medical Kit and Medicines](../medical/medical-kit.md).
- For stings and bites, see [Bites, Stings and Venom](../medical/bites-stings.md).
