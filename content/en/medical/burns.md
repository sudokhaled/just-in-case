> [!CAUTION]
> **Cool the burn with cool running water for 20 minutes.** Do this as soon as possible. It still helps up to 3 hours after the burn. Never use ice, butter, oil or toothpaste.
>
> **Burns of the face, soot around the nose or mouth, or a hoarse voice can mean burns inside the airway.** The throat can swell shut over the next hours. Get help urgently.
>
> **Turn off the power before you touch someone with an electrical injury.**

## Quick steps

1. **Stop the burning.** Get the person away from the heat. If clothes are on fire: stop, drop to the ground, and roll. Smother flames with a heavy cotton or wool blanket.
2. **Cool the burn** under cool running water for **20 minutes.** Lukewarm or cool tap water is right. Not ice.
3. **Remove rings, watches, belts and tight clothing** near the burn before it swells. Do not pull off anything stuck to the skin.
4. **Cover loosely** with cling film (plastic wrap) laid on in strips, a clean plastic bag, or a clean cloth that does not shed fibers.
5. **Keep the person warm.** Cool only the burn, not the whole body.
6. **Get help** for large burns, burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals or joints, burns all the way around a limb, electrical or chemical burns, smoke breathed in, and burns in babies or older people.

## Cooling

- Use cool or lukewarm running water from a tap, a hose, or a container poured slowly. The best temperature is cool, not cold.
- Keep it up for **20 minutes.** Shorter cooling helps less.
- If water is scarce, soak clean cloths in cool water and lay them on the burn. Change them every minute or so as they warm.
- **Do not use ice or ice water.** They damage the skin further.
- **Watch for hypothermia,** especially in babies, small children, older people and anyone with a large burn. Cover the rest of the body. Stop cooling if they start shivering or feel cold.

## Covering

- Lay **cling film (plastic wrap)** on the burn in strips. Do not wrap it tightly around a limb: the burn will swell.
- A clean plastic bag works well for a burned hand.
- **Do not put cling film on the face.** Keep cooling the face with wet cloths.
- Do not use cotton wool or fluffy cloth. The fibers stick to the burn.
- Cling film is for the first hours. For longer care, see below.

## How serious is it?

### Size

The person's own **palm with the fingers together is about 1%** of their body surface. Count how many palms the burn would cover. Do not count skin that is only red.

| Body part | Adult | Baby or small child |
|---|---|---|
| Head and neck | 9% | 18% |
| Each arm | 9% | 9% |
| Front of the body | 18% | 18% |
| Back of the body | 18% | 18% |
| Each leg | 18% | 14% |

In adults, the genitals count as about 1%. For babies and small children the numbers are approximate: the head is a much larger part of the body and the legs a smaller part.

### Depth

| Depth | What it looks like | Pain | Healing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial | Red, dry, no blisters, like sunburn | Painful | About 1 week, no scar |
| Partial thickness | Blisters, wet, pink or red | Very painful | 2-3 weeks if shallow; deeper ones take longer and scar |
| Full thickness | White, waxy, leathery, brown or black | Little or none in the center | Does not heal well alone; needs surgery (skin graft) |

A full-thickness burn may not hurt, because the nerves are destroyed. **No pain does not mean a minor burn.**

### When a burn is serious

Get medical help, or evacuate if help cannot come, for:

- partial or full thickness burns larger than **10% of the body in adults** or **5% in children**
- any full-thickness burn bigger than a coin
- burns of the **face, hands, feet, genitals** or **over major joints**
- burns that go **all the way around** an arm, leg, neck or chest. They can cut off blood flow or breathing as they swell.
- **airway burns:** burned in a closed space, soot around the nose or mouth, singed nose hairs, a hoarse voice, coughing or noisy breathing
- **electrical** and **chemical** burns
- burns in babies, older people, pregnant women, or people with other injuries or illness

People caught in a fire may also have carbon monoxide poisoning: headache, confusion, vomiting, drowsiness. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).

## Chemical burns

1. Protect yourself with gloves or plastic bags.
2. **Brush off dry powder** (such as lime or cement) before adding water.
3. Remove contaminated clothing and jewelry.
4. **Flush with lots of running water for at least 20 minutes.** Keep going if the pain continues.
5. **Eyes:** hold the eyelids open and flush for at least 20 minutes, from the inner corner outwards, so the water does not run into the other eye. Remove contact lenses. See [Eye Injuries](../medical/eye-injuries.md).
6. Do not try to neutralize an acid with an alkali or the other way round. It makes heat and worse burns.

## Electrical burns

1. **Turn off the power** at the switch, plug or main fuse box before touching the person.
2. If you cannot turn it off, and it is ordinary house power, stand on dry wood, rubber or newspaper and push the person away from the source with a dry wooden broom handle.
3. **High-voltage power lines:** stay at least 18 m (20 yards) away until the power company says the line is dead.
4. Check breathing. Start CPR if needed. See [CPR: When Someone Is Not Breathing](../medical/cpr.md).
5. Cool and cover the burns.

Electrical burns often look small on the skin but damage deep tissue and can upset the heart rhythm. **Everyone with an electrical injury needs a medical check,** even if they feel fine.

## Fluids

Large burns make the body lose a lot of fluid, which causes shock. If a burn is larger than about **10% in an adult or 5% in a child,** and the person is awake, can swallow and is not vomiting, give **oral rehydration solution (ORS)** in small, frequent sips. Aim for pale yellow urine every few hours. See [Dehydration, Diarrhea and Oral Rehydration](../medical/dehydration.md).

## When no hospital can be reached

1. Clean the burn gently with clean, drinkable water. Wash the skin around it with mild soap.
2. **Leave blisters closed.** If one bursts, wash it gently and cover it.
3. Cover with a thin layer of petroleum jelly, or medical-grade honey if you have it, and a non-stick dressing. Then add gauze or clean cloth.
4. Change the dressing **every 1-2 days.** Soak it off with clean water if it sticks.
5. Raise burned arms and legs to reduce swelling.
6. For pain, give paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen as the package says.
7. **Move burned joints** gently several times a day once the pain allows, so the skin does not heal tight and stiff.
8. Give plenty of food, especially protein such as eggs, beans, meat or fish. Large burns need much more food than normal.
9. Watch for infection: spreading redness, more pain, swelling, pus, a bad smell, or fever. See [Fever, Infection and Sepsis](../medical/infections-fever.md).

Burns are tetanus-prone. Check tetanus vaccination as soon as you can. See [Cleaning and Caring for Wounds](../medical/wounds.md).

**Sunburn:** get out of the sun, cool the skin with water, drink plenty, and use a moisturizing cream or aloe gel. Blistering sunburn over a large area, with fever or vomiting, needs medical care.

## Common mistakes and myths

- **Butter, oil or grease:** trap heat and cause infection. Never use them.
- **Ice:** freezes and damages the skin.
- **Toothpaste, egg white, flour, mud, soy sauce, coffee or herbs:** do not help, cause infection, and must be scrubbed off painfully later.
- **Popping blisters:** opens the burn to infection.
- **Peeling off stuck clothing:** tears the skin. Cut around it.
- **"Cooling is only useful in the first minutes."** Cooling helps for up to 3 hours.
- **"It does not hurt, so it is not bad."** The deepest burns often do not hurt.
