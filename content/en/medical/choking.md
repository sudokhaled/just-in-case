> [!CAUTION]
> **If they can cough, let them cough.** Do not hit someone who is still coughing hard and breathing. A strong cough clears the airway better than anything you can do.
>
> **Never push your fingers blindly into the mouth to search.** You can push the object deeper. Only remove something you can see and grab easily.
>
> **Never give abdominal thrusts to a baby under 1 year.** Use back blows and chest thrusts.

## Quick steps (adults and children over 1 year)

1. **Ask:** "Are you choking?" If they can speak, cough and breathe, tell them to keep coughing. Stay with them.
2. **If they cannot speak, cough or breathe,** or the cough is getting weak: give up to **5 back blows.**
3. **If that does not work:** give up to **5 abdominal thrusts.**
4. **Keep alternating** 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or they collapse.
5. **Get someone to call** emergency services as soon as possible.
6. **If they stop responding,** lower them to the ground and start CPR.

## Mild or severe?

| Sign | Mild choking (partly blocked) | Severe choking (fully blocked) |
|---|---|---|
| Speaking or crying | Can speak or cry | Cannot speak or make a sound |
| Cough | Loud and strong | Silent or weak |
| Breathing | Breathing | Not breathing, or a high-pitched noise |
| Color | Normal | Lips and face turning blue or grey |

People who are choking often grab their throat. Mild choking can turn severe. Stay with them and watch.

## Back blows

1. Stand to the side and slightly behind them. For a child, kneel behind them.
2. Support their chest with one hand. Lean them well forward, so the object falls out of the mouth and not deeper.
3. Hit firmly between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand.
4. Give up to 5 blows. Check after each one whether the object has come out.

A small child can be laid across your lap, face down, head lower than the chest, for back blows.

## Abdominal thrusts

1. Stand behind them. Put both arms around the top part of their belly. Lean them forward.
2. Make a fist. Put it, thumb side in, between the belly button and the bottom of the breastbone.
3. Hold your fist with your other hand.
4. Pull sharply **inwards and upwards.**
5. Give up to 5 thrusts. Check after each one.

For a child, kneel behind them and use less force.

### Pregnant or very large people

If you cannot get your arms around the belly, or the woman is clearly pregnant, use **chest thrusts** instead.

1. Stand behind them. Put your arms under their armpits, around the chest.
2. Put your fist on the center of the breastbone, the same place as for CPR.
3. Hold your fist with your other hand and pull sharply **backwards** towards you.
4. Give up to 5 chest thrusts, alternating with 5 back blows.

## Babies under 1 year

### Back blows

1. Sit or kneel. Lay the baby face down along your forearm, with your arm resting on your thigh.
2. Keep the head **lower than the chest.** Support the head by holding the jaw with your thumb and fingers. Do not press on the soft throat.
3. Give up to **5 firm back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. Check after each one.

### Chest thrusts

1. Place your free hand along the baby's back and hold the back of the head.
2. Turn the baby over onto your other forearm, face up. Keep the head lower than the chest.
3. Put the **heel of one hand** on the lower half of the breastbone, just below a line between the nipples. (Older courses taught 2 fingers. Current guidance uses the heel of the hand, because fingers do not press deep enough.)
4. Give up to **5 chest thrusts.** Press down about 4 cm (1.5 in), one-third of the chest depth. Make each thrust sharper and slower than a CPR compression.
5. Look in the mouth. Remove an object only if you can see it and pick it out easily.
6. Repeat 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until it clears or the baby stops responding.

Do not hold a baby upside down by the feet and shake it. Do not shake a baby at all.

## If they collapse

1. Lower them carefully to the floor.
2. Call emergency services, or get someone to call. Put the phone on speaker.
3. **Start CPR with chest compressions,** even if they are still trying to breathe. Compressions can push the object out.
4. **Before each set of rescue breaths, open the mouth and look.** Remove the object only if you can see it and grab it easily.
5. For a child or a baby, start with 5 rescue breaths.

See [CPR: When Someone Is Not Breathing](../medical/cpr.md).

## Choking when you are alone

1. If a phone is in your hand, call emergency services and leave the line open. Do not waste time searching for one.
2. Make a fist and put it above your belly button. Hold it with your other hand. Thrust it hard **inwards and upwards.**
3. Or lean over the back of a chair, the edge of a table or a railing. Throw your upper belly hard against it. Repeat until the object comes out.
4. Move to where other people can see you.

## After choking

- **Anyone who had abdominal thrusts or chest thrusts should see a doctor if possible.** The thrusts can injure organs inside the belly and chest.
- A cough that does not stop, wheezing, pain when swallowing, or a feeling that something is still stuck can mean part of the object went into the lungs. This needs medical care. It can cause pneumonia days later.
- **When no doctor can be reached,** watch the person for the next 2 days. Evacuate if they have belly pain that gets worse, vomit blood, have a fever, or breathe fast. See [Fever, Infection and Sepsis](../medical/infections-fever.md).

## Prevention

- **Children under 5:** cut round foods such as grapes, cherry tomatoes and sausages **lengthwise into quarters.** Do not give whole nuts, popcorn or hard sweets. Make children sit down while they eat.
- Keep coins, small toys, magnets and **button batteries** away from young children. A swallowed button battery can burn through the food pipe within 2 hours. Swallowed magnets can also cause serious damage. Both need a doctor urgently. See [Poisoning](../medical/poisoning.md).
- **Adults:** cut food small, chew well, and do not talk or laugh with a full mouth. Alcohol makes choking more likely.
- **Older people and people with swallowing problems:** give soft, moist food. Help them sit upright to eat.
- For safe first foods for babies, see [Feeding Babies and Young Children in an Emergency](../food/feeding-babies.md).

## Is it choking or something else?

- **Swelling of the lips, tongue or throat,** often with an itchy rash, after food, a sting or medicine, is a severe allergic reaction. See [Severe Allergic Reactions (Anaphylaxis)](../medical/allergic-reactions.md).
- **A child with a fever, drooling and noisy breathing** may have a swollen throat from infection. Do not look in the throat or put anything in the mouth. Keep the child calm and sitting up, and get help urgently.
