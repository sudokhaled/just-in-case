> [!CAUTION]
> **Do not seal an open chest wound airtight.** Do not tape plastic, foil or a bag over it, on three sides or on all sides. Air can build up inside the chest, squeeze the lungs and heart, and kill. Leave the wound open, or cover it loosely with cloth.
>
> **Do not pull out an object stuck in the chest or belly.** It may be plugging a bleeding blood vessel.
>
> **Do not push organs back into a belly wound.** Cover them with a clean, damp cloth.

## Quick steps

1. **Make the scene safe.** Send someone to call emergency services.
2. **Find every wound.** Cut away clothing. Check the back, sides and armpits: a bullet or blade can make a second wound.
3. **Stop bleeding** by pressing a cloth on top of the wound with your hand. **Do not stuff cloth deep into a chest or belly wound.**
4. **Leave a chest wound open,** or cover it loosely with a cloth that air can pass through.
5. **Chest injury, awake:** help them sit up, leaning toward the injured side.
6. **Belly injury:** lay them on their back with the knees bent and supported.
7. **Give nothing to eat or drink** if help can reach them within hours.
8. **Check breathing every 5 minutes.** If breathing gets worse, evacuate now.

## Open chest wounds

A wound through the chest wall lets air in around the lung. You may hear sucking, or see blood bubbling at the wound, as they breathe. The lung on that side can collapse.

1. Press on top of the wound with a cloth only as hard as needed to control bleeding.
2. Leave the wound open, or cover it loosely with gauze or a folded cloth that air can pass through.
3. Help them sit up, leaning toward the injured side. This keeps the healthy lung on top, where it works best.
4. If they stop responding but still breathe normally, put them in the recovery position with the **injured side down.** See [First Aid Basics: Check, Call, Care](../medical/first-aid-basics.md).
5. If they stop breathing normally, start CPR.

> [!WARNING]
> A blood-soaked dressing can become airtight. If breathing gets worse after you cover the wound, lift the dressing off for a moment so trapped air can escape. Then put it back loosely.

Older first aid books taught a plastic seal taped on 3 sides. Current guidelines no longer recommend it for first aiders. Trained medics may use a commercial vented chest seal with a one-way valve.

## Danger signs: collapsed lung or bleeding in the chest

These can also follow a fall, kick or crash with no wound:

- breathing that gets faster, harder or more painful
- one side of the chest rising less than the other, or not at all
- blue or grey lips, tongue or face
- a pulse that keeps rising, with pale, cold, sweaty skin (shock)
- restlessness, confusion or growing drowsiness
- coughing up bright red or frothy blood
- swelling under the skin of the chest or neck that crackles when pressed

**Evacuate at once** if any appear. Only trained staff can release trapped air or blood. **Do not push a needle, knife or tube into the chest yourself.**

## Broken ribs

**Signs:** sharp pain at one spot, worse when breathing in, coughing or moving; pain when you press gently on the rib.

1. **Do not strap or tie the chest tightly.** It stops deep breaths and leads to pneumonia.
2. Give pain relief, such as paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen, as the package says. Good pain control lets them breathe deeply. Avoid ibuprofen and aspirin if there may be bleeding inside the body.
3. **Every hour while awake, take about 10 slow, deep breaths and cough.** Hugging a folded blanket or pillow against the ribs makes coughing hurt less.
4. Sit up, and sleep propped up for the first few nights. Walk gently when possible: lying still raises the risk of pneumonia.

Most broken ribs heal in about 6 weeks.

**Get help urgently if** breathing gets harder, they cough up blood, a fever starts with a cough and colored phlegm (pneumonia), pain spreads to the belly or shoulder tip, or several ribs are broken. Older people are at higher risk. Broken lower ribs can injure the liver (right) or spleen (left).

### Flail chest

When several ribs are each broken in 2 or more places, a piece of the chest wall comes loose. It sinks in when the person breathes in and bulges out when they breathe out. This is life-threatening.

1. Help them sit up leaning toward the injured side, or lie on the injured side if that is easier.
2. Let them hold a folded cloth or small pillow against the loose part if this eases the pain. Do not tie anything tightly around the chest.
3. Give pain relief as above. **Evacuate urgently.**

## Objects stuck in the chest or belly

1. **Leave it in.** Do not pull, wiggle or push it.
2. If it bleeds, press around it, not on it.
3. Pad around the object on all sides with rolled cloth. Bandage over the pads so it cannot move.
4. Shorten it only if you must, to move the person, while someone holds it completely still.
5. Keep the person still and evacuate.

## Belly wounds with organs outside

1. **Do not push the organs back,** and touch them as little as possible.
2. Cover them with a large clean cloth soaked in clean water (boiled and cooled, or treated).
3. Cover the wet cloth with clean plastic wrap, a plastic bag or foil to keep it moist and warm. Tape it loosely.
4. Lay them on their back with the knees bent over a rolled blanket. This relaxes the belly muscles.
5. Keep them warm and treat for shock. Wet the cloth again if it starts to dry.

**Every wound into the belly needs a surgeon, even a small one.** A cut bowel leaks and causes deadly infection within a day or 2.

## Blunt belly injury and hidden bleeding

A kick, fall, crash, seat belt or crush can tear the liver, spleen or kidneys with no wound on the skin. Bleeding inside can be slow, so signs may appear hours later.

Suspect bleeding inside the belly if there is:

- belly pain or tenderness that gets worse
- a belly that is swollen, or hard and tight like a board
- bruising across the belly, lower ribs or sides, such as a seat belt mark
- pain in the tip of the left shoulder (often from a torn spleen)
- signs of shock with no visible bleeding
- blood in the urine or vomit

Lay them down, keep them warm, give nothing by mouth if help can come within hours, and **evacuate urgently.** After any hard blow to the belly, watch them closely for at least 24 hours, even if they seem fine. See [Stopping Severe Bleeding](../medical/severe-bleeding.md). A broken pelvis also bleeds heavily inside: see [Broken Bones, Sprains and Dislocations](../medical/fractures-sprains.md).

## Crush injury to the chest

When a heavy weight or a crowd presses on the chest, the person cannot breathe.

1. **Remove the weight at once.** Unlike an arm or leg trapped for hours, a squeezed chest must be freed immediately.
2. Check breathing. Not breathing normally: start CPR.
3. If they breathe, help them sit half-up, unless you suspect a spine injury.
4. The face, neck and upper chest may turn deep purple, with tiny red dots and red eyes. This usually fades over days. The real danger is injury to the ribs, lungs and heart.
5. Evacuate. See [Crowds, Crushes and Stampedes](../disasters/crowd-crush.md).

## When help is days away

- Chest injuries: keep them in the position that makes breathing easiest, usually half-sitting.
- Count breathing and pulse every 15 minutes at first, then every hour, and write them down. Rising numbers mean they are getting worse.
- Change dressings every day and watch for infection. See [Cleaning and Caring for Wounds](../medical/wounds.md).
- **Fluids:** if evacuation will take days and they are fully awake and not vomiting, give small sips of clean water or oral rehydration solution often. Evidence is limited, but days without water also kill. Stop if they vomit or the belly swells.

## Evacuate if

- there is any wound into the chest or belly, however small
- breathing is difficult or getting worse, or one side of the chest does not move
- there is a flail chest, or several broken ribs
- they cough up or vomit blood
- the belly is hard, swollen or more and more painful
- there are signs of shock
- an object is stuck in the body, or organs are outside the belly
- the chest was crushed

Carry chest injuries half-sitting, and belly injuries lying on the back with the knees bent, unless a spine injury is possible. See [Moving and Carrying the Injured](../medical/moving-injured.md).
