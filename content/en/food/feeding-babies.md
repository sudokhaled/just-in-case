> [!CAUTION]
> **Breastfeeding is the safest way to feed a baby in an emergency. Do not stop.** Stress does not dry up breast milk. Feeding more often makes more milk.
>
> **Never add extra water to formula to make it last.** Watered-down formula starves a baby. Too little water can dehydrate a baby. Use the exact amounts on the package.
>
> **Avoid bottles and teats.** They are very hard to clean and can cause deadly diarrhea. Feed with a clean, open cup.
>
> **Do not give a baby under 6 months animal milk, tea, juice, sugar water or rice water** unless there is truly nothing else.

## Quick steps

1. **Breastfeed whenever the baby wants,** day and night. Young babies need at least 8 feeds in 24 hours.
2. **Give a baby under 6 months only breast milk.** No water, tea, juice or food, even in hot weather.
3. **Feed and protect the mother:** extra food and drink, rest, privacy and support.
4. **If a mother has stopped or has little milk,** put the baby to the breast often to restart it. Look for another breastfeeding woman who can help.
5. **If formula is the only option,** use ready-to-use liquid formula first. Make powdered formula only with boiled water and exact measures, and feed by cup.
6. **From 6 months,** keep breastfeeding and add thick, soft foods 2 or 3 times a day.
7. **Watch for dehydration** if a baby has diarrhea. See [Dehydration, Diarrhea and Oral Rehydration](../medical/dehydration.md).

## Why breastfeeding matters most

In emergencies, babies who are not breastfed are far more likely to die from diarrhea and infections. Breast milk:

- Is always clean and at the right temperature. It needs no water, fuel or washing up.
- Has all the water a baby under 6 months needs, even in hot climates.
- Protects against diarrhea, pneumonia and other infections.
- Comforts the baby and keeps it warm through skin-to-skin contact.

Keep breastfeeding until 2 years of age or longer. Breastfeeding prevents pregnancy only under strict conditions: see [Family Planning and Contraception](../medical/contraception.md).

## Keeping the milk flowing

Fear and stress can slow the flow of milk for a while, but they do not stop the body from making it. The more often the baby sucks, the more milk the body makes.

- Feed often, whenever the baby shows hunger. Do not follow a fixed schedule.
- Keep the baby on the mother's bare chest as much as possible.
- To help milk flow, give the mother a quiet, private place, a warm drink, a warm cloth on the breasts, gentle breast massage, and someone she trusts nearby.
- A thin or hungry mother can still make enough milk. Give her about 500 kcal of extra food a day, and let her drink whenever she is thirsty.
- **Signs the baby is getting enough** (after the first week): at least 6 wet diapers (nappies) in 24 hours, pale urine, regular stools, and an alert baby.
- If a baby is getting some formula, breastfeed more often and slowly reduce the formula.

Keep mothers and babies together. Give them priority for shelter, food, water and privacy.

## Restarting breast milk (relactation)

A woman who stopped breastfeeding, even weeks or months ago, can often make milk again. Some grandmothers and adoptive mothers have done this.

1. Put the baby to the breast often: at least 8 to 12 times in 24 hours, including at night.
2. Keep the baby skin-to-skin as much as possible.
3. While milk is low, give the baby other milk at the same time. Tape a thin, clean tube to the breast with the other end in a cup of milk, so the baby gets milk while sucking. Or give milk by cup after each breastfeed.
4. Keep going. Some milk often returns within a few days to 2 weeks. A full supply can take longer.
5. Reduce the other milk slowly as the breast milk increases.

The mother needs encouragement, food, rest and someone to help her.

## Wet nursing

Another woman who is breastfeeding can feed the baby. This is a safe choice when the mother is absent, has died or is very ill.

- If testing is available, the wet nurse should be tested for HIV.
- She needs extra food and support.

A mother who is living with HIV should follow advice from a health worker. In most situations, WHO advises her to keep breastfeeding and keep taking her HIV medicines.

## If formula is unavoidable

Formula is needed when there is no mother or wet nurse, when the mother is very sick, or when the baby was already fully formula fed and restarting breast milk is not possible.

Safest to least safe:

1. Ready-to-use liquid infant formula. No mixing, no water needed.
2. Powdered infant formula made with boiled water.
3. Home-modified animal milk, only as a last resort (see below).

Use only **infant formula** for babies under 6 months, not "follow-on", "growing-up" or toddler milks. Plan for a steady supply until the baby is at least 6 months old.

### Ready-to-use liquid formula

- Pour it into a clean cup. Do not add water.
- Without a fridge, use an opened carton for 1 feed only. Small single-feed cartons are best.
- Throw away anything left within 2 hours of opening.

### Powdered formula

Powdered formula is not sterile. It can contain dangerous germs. Water at **70°C (158°F) or hotter** kills them.

1. Wash your hands with soap and safe water. Clean the surface you work on.
2. Bring safe water to a rolling boil.
3. Let it cool for **no more than 30 minutes,** so it stays at 70°C (158°F) or hotter.
4. Pour the exact amount of water shown on the package into a clean cup or container.
5. Add the exact number of **level** scoops of powder. Use only the scoop from that package.
6. Mix well.
7. Cool it quickly to body temperature: stand the closed container in a bowl of cold safe water, or hold it under cool running water.
8. Test a few drops on the inside of your wrist. It should feel lukewarm, not hot.
9. Feed the baby with a cup.
10. **Throw away any formula not used within 2 hours.** Make a fresh feed each time.

Never add sugar, cereal, honey or anything else to formula.

### Cup feeding

Cups are much easier to clean than bottles. Even newborns and small babies can feed from a cup.

1. Use a small, clean cup.
2. Hold the baby upright on your lap, supporting the back and neck.
3. Rest the cup on the baby's lower lip, with the edge touching the upper lip.
4. Tip the cup just until the milk touches the baby's lips. The baby will sip or lap.
5. **Never pour milk into the baby's mouth.** Let the baby feed at its own pace and pause when it pauses.
6. Wash the cup with soap and safe water after each feed. Boil it if you can.

### Animal milk (last resort)

For a baby under 6 months, fresh animal milk (cow, goat, buffalo, sheep, camel) is risky. It has too much protein and salt for the baby's kidneys, too little iron and vitamins, and can cause bleeding in the gut, anemia and dehydration. Use it only when there is no breast milk, no wet nurse and no infant formula.

If you must, WHO has described this short-term mix for cow's or goat's milk:

1. Mix 100 mL of fresh milk with 50 mL of safe water and 10 g (2 level teaspoons) of sugar.
2. Bring it to the boil, then cool it to lukewarm.
3. Feed by cup. Make it fresh for each feed.
4. Find breast milk or infant formula as soon as possible.

This mix lacks iron and several vitamins that babies need. WHO pairs it with a micronutrient supplement. Ask any health worker or aid agency for one, and use the mix for as short a time as possible.

**Never use** condensed milk, skimmed milk, coffee creamer, plant milks (soy, rice, almond), tea, juice or sugar water to feed a baby under 6 months. **Never give honey to a baby under 1 year.** It can cause botulism.

## Feeding children from 6 months

From 6 months, breast milk alone is not enough. Keep breastfeeding and add other food.

| Age | Meals per day | Amount at each meal |
|---|---|---|
| 6 to 8 months | 2 to 3 meals, plus breastfeeds | Start with 2 to 3 spoonfuls. Increase to half a 250 mL (1 cup) bowl. |
| 9 to 11 months | 3 to 4 meals, plus 1 to 2 snacks | Half a 250 mL (1 cup) bowl |
| 12 to 23 months | 3 to 4 meals, plus 1 to 2 snacks | Three-quarters to 1 full 250 mL (1 cup) bowl |

What to give:

- **Thick porridge** that stays on a spoon. Watery porridge or soup fills the stomach with too little food.
- **Mashed family food:** grains, beans, lentils, vegetables and fruit. Add a little oil or butter for energy.
- **Animal foods every day if possible:** egg, meat, fish, liver or boiled milk.
- **Avoid:** honey before 1 year, whole nuts and hard pieces (choking), sweet drinks.

How to give it:

- Wash your hands and the child's hands. Use clean bowls and spoons.
- Make food fresh, and eat it within 2 hours. See [Food Safety Without a Fridge](../food/food-safety.md).
- Help the child eat. Be patient. Do not force food.
- When a child is sick, keep feeding, and give more fluids and breastfeeds. After the illness, give more food than usual, such as 1 extra meal a day, until the child is back to normal weight and energy. See [Malnutrition in Children and Adults](../medical/malnutrition.md).

## Dehydration in babies

Babies dehydrate quickly with diarrhea, vomiting or heat. Signs:

- Fewer wet diapers than usual, or dark urine.
- Dry mouth, and crying without tears.
- A sunken soft spot on top of the head, or sunken eyes.
- Very sleepy, floppy or hard to wake: this is an emergency.

Keep breastfeeding, more often than usual. Give oral rehydration solution by cup or spoon, in small sips. See [Dehydration, Diarrhea and Oral Rehydration](../medical/dehydration.md). For safe water, see [Making Water Safe to Drink](../water/purify-water.md). For keeping children calm and safe, see [Keeping Children Safe and Calm](../community/children-safety.md).
