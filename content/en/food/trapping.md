> [!CAUTION]
> **Never eat an animal that looked sick, acted strangely, or was found dead. Never touch or eat bats, monkeys or apes.** They can carry rabies, Ebola and other deadly diseases.
>
> **Wear gloves when you gut and skin.** If a rabbit's or hare's liver has white or yellow spots, bury or burn the whole animal and wash well: it may have tularemia (rabbit fever).
>
> **Cook all wild meat until no pink remains**, to 71°C (160°F) inside. Bears, wild pigs and meat-eating animals carry worms (trichinella) that cooking kills and freezing may not.

## Quick steps

1. **Find animal trails:** narrow paths through grass, droppings, tracks, chewed plants and burrow entrances.
2. **Set many snares,** 10 or more, where trails squeeze through narrow gaps.
3. **Check every snare at least once a day,** best at first light.
4. **Kill caught animals quickly** with a hard blow to the back of the head.
5. **Gut and skin with gloves.** Check the liver. Keep the fat and healthy organs.
6. **Cook thoroughly.** Eat fat or plant food with lean meat.

## Why trapping beats hunting

Chasing or stalking animals burns about 250 to 450 kcal per hour and often fails. A snare takes a few minutes to set and works day and night while you rest. A wild rabbit gives roughly 400 to 800 kcal of meat; a squirrel roughly 200 to 300 kcal. Most snares catch nothing on most nights, so set many and expect a low success rate.

## Where to set traps

- **Trails:** small animals use the same paths every day. Look for flattened grass, worn soil, droppings and tracks.
- **Burrows and dens:** fresh droppings and dug soil at the entrance show the burrow is in use.
- **Natural funnels:** where a trail passes between rocks, under a log, through a hedge or a hole in a fence.
- **Make a funnel:** push sticks or brush into the ground on both sides of a trail so the animal must pass through your snare.
- **Near water and food:** stream banks, the edges of fields and berry bushes.
- **Disturb as little as possible.** Do not trample the trail. Rub your hands with soil before setting to hide your smell.

## Simple snare

A snare is a loop that tightens around the animal's neck as it walks through.

1. **Use wire** if you can: thin brass or steel wire. Animals chew through string and cord.
2. **Make a small eye** at one end: bend the wire around a nail or twig and twist it tightly.
3. **Pass the other end through the eye** to form a loop that slides closed easily.
4. **Size the loop:** for rabbits and hares, about **10 cm (4 in) across** (a fist), with the bottom about **10 cm (4 in), a hand's width, above the ground.** For squirrels, about 6 cm (2.5 in) across.
5. **Anchor it firmly** to a strong stake, root or tree. A caught animal pulls hard.
6. **Hold the loop open and upright** across the trail with thin twigs pushed into the ground.

For squirrels, lean a long pole against a tree they use, and set several small snares along the top of the pole.

### Legal and humane notes

- **Snares are illegal in many countries,** and licensed or tightly controlled in others. In normal times, check the law. In a true emergency, survival comes first.
- **Check every snare at least once every 24 hours.** An animal left in a snare suffers and may be eaten by predators.
- **Snares catch pets and protected animals too.** Release them if you can do it safely. Do not set snares near homes or paths people use.
- **Count your snares and remove every one when you leave.** A forgotten snare keeps killing.

## Deadfalls: the idea and the danger

A deadfall is a heavy rock or log propped up by a trigger made of sticks. When an animal moves the bait, the trigger slips and the weight falls on it. Common designs are the figure-4 and the Paiute deadfall.

- **Deadfalls injure the people who set them.** The weight falls when you least expect it. Never put your hands, arms or head under the weight. Set the trigger from the side, with the weight resting on the ground first if possible.
- The weight must be several times heavier than the animal, or it only injures it.
- Never build deadfalls big enough to hurt a person, and never set them where children play or people walk.

## Preparing small game

1. **Put on gloves,** or plastic bags over your hands. Cover any cuts. Avoid splashes to your eyes.
2. **Gut it soon after killing,** before the meat spoils. Lay the animal on its back. Pinch up the belly skin and cut a small slit, without cutting into the guts.
3. **Open the belly** from the hips to the ribs, with 2 fingers under the skin lifting it away from the guts.
4. **Pull out the guts.** Do not burst the bladder or intestines. If their contents touch the meat, cut that part away and rinse with safe water.
5. **Check the liver.** A healthy liver is dark red-brown, smooth and firm. **White or yellow spots mean disease:** do not eat any of the animal. Bury or burn it.
6. **Skin it.** For rabbits, cut a ring around each back leg, then peel the skin toward the head like pulling off a sock. Cut off the head and feet.
7. **Keep the heart, liver and kidneys** if they look healthy, and all fat. Rinse and cool the meat.
8. **Wash your hands, knife and gloves** with soap and safe water. See [Handwashing and Personal Hygiene](../hygiene/handwashing.md).

Use the guts as bait for fishing or traps, or bury them far from your shelter.

### Disease risks

- **Tularemia (rabbit fever)** spreads from rabbits, hares and rodents through cuts, eye splashes and undercooked meat. Fever, a skin ulcer and swollen glands usually start 3 to 5 days later (up to 14 days). It needs antibiotics. A clean-looking liver does not guarantee safety, so always use gloves and cook well.
- **Rodents (rats, mice):** can carry leptospirosis, hantavirus and plague-carrying fleas. Do not breathe dust from nests. Fleas leave a dead animal as it cools, so handle it with gloves and keep it away from your clothes.
- **Bats, monkeys and apes:** never eat or handle them. They carry rabies, Ebola, Marburg and other viruses. Butchering, not just eating, spreads these diseases.
- **Foxes, raccoons, skunks, dogs:** can carry rabies. Avoid them unless there is no other food.

## Cooking

- **Cook until 71°C (160°F) inside.** Without a thermometer: no pink meat, clear juices, and meat that pulls easily off the bone.
- **Trichinella worms** live in bears, wild pigs, walrus, seals, foxes and other meat-eating animals. Smoking, drying, salting and freezing do **not** reliably kill them in wild meat. Only full cooking does.
- **Stew or boil small game.** It keeps the fat and juices, and the broth is food. Cut meat into small pieces so it cooks evenly.
- To dry or smoke meat for later, see [Preserving Food Without Power](../food/food-preservation.md).

## Rabbit starvation: lean meat is not enough

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A person who eats only lean meat, such as rabbit or squirrel, can become sick within about a week, even with a full stomach. The body cannot safely get more than about a third of its energy from protein. Signs are nausea, diarrhea, weakness, headache and a constant craving for fat. It can kill.

To prevent it:

- **Eat fat.** Keep and eat all fat, bone marrow (crack the long bones) and organ meats. Fish and fatty animals help.
- **Add carbohydrates.** Grain, beans, potatoes, acorns and starchy roots. See [Foraging Wild Plants Safely](../food/foraging.md).
- **Do not rely on lean meat alone** for more than a few days.
