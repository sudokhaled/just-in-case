> [!CAUTION]
> **If you are not 100% sure what a plant is, do not eat it.** One bite of water hemlock root can kill an adult within hours.
>
> **Never eat wild mushrooms.** Death cap mushrooms look ordinary and taste pleasant. Symptoms start 6 to 24 hours later, then the liver fails. Cooking does not destroy the poison. No color, smell or peeling test tells safe from deadly.
>
> **Never eat any plant with umbrella-shaped flower heads** (many tiny flowers on stalks spreading from one point) **or leaves like carrot, parsley or dill.** This family (Apiaceae) includes poison hemlock and water hemlock, which look almost exactly like wild carrot.

## Quick steps

1. **Eat stored food first.** Most wild greens give very few calories. They add vitamins; they rarely replace a meal.
2. **Learn 3 to 5 plants well** from this article. Check leaf, stem, flower, root, smell, place, and every look-alike. If 2 people disagree, do not eat it.
3. **Pick in clean places,** away from roads, railways, sprayed land and polluted water.
4. **Wash in safe water and cook** when you can. Always cook water plants: they can carry parasitic worms.
5. **Try one new plant at a time.** Eat a few bites, then wait 24 hours before eating more.
6. **If anyone feels ill,** stop, keep a sample of the plant, and see [Poisoning](../medical/poisoning.md).

Teach children to eat nothing wild without asking an adult. They are the most common victims of berry poisoning.

## Conservative warning signs

The US Army survival manual says to avoid plants with: milky or colored sap; beans, bulbs or seeds in pods; a bitter or soapy taste; spines, fine hairs or thorns; leaves like dill, carrot or parsley; an almond smell in crushed leaves or wood (cyanide); grain heads with pink, purple or black spurs (ergot mold); or 3 leaflets, like poison ivy.

These rules reject many safe plants on purpose. Dandelion (milky sap), nettle (stinging hairs), clover (3 leaflets) and blackberry (thorns) break them.

> [!WARNING]
> Passing these rules does **not** make a plant safe. Many poisonous plants, including deadly nightshade and yew, pass every rule.

## The universal edibility test: last resort only

This US Army test takes **more than 24 hours** per plant part. It does **not** protect you from the deadliest plants: the small amount swallowed can kill with hemlock, and death cap gives no warning for hours. Use it only when you have no known food, no rescue is coming and the plant is plentiful. Never test mushrooms or plants with the warning signs above.

1. Choose one part (leaf, stem, root, flower or seed). Eat nothing for 8 hours before.
2. Rub it on the inside of your wrist for 15 minutes. Stop if it burns, itches or makes a rash.
3. Prepare a small piece the way you will eat it. Touch it to your lip for 3 minutes, then hold it on your tongue for 15 minutes.
4. Chew a pinch and hold it in your mouth for 15 minutes without swallowing. Stop at any burning, numbness, or bitter or soapy taste.
5. Swallow the pinch. Wait 8 hours, taking only safe water.
6. If you feel well, eat 1/4 cup (60 mL) prepared the same way and wait 8 more hours. If you still feel well, that part, prepared that way, is probably safe.

If you feel sick at any step, stop, spit out what is in your mouth and rinse with water.

## Plants that are hard to mistake

These grow on most continents, mostly in temperate regions. Not all grow everywhere. For tropical staples such as banana, cassava, sago and palm hearts, see [Living in a Tropical Rainforest](../land/jungle-living.md).

### Dandelion

**Identify:** a flat ring of hairless, deeply toothed leaves. Each hollow, leafless, unbranched stem carries **one** yellow flower. Stems leak white milky sap.

**Look-alikes:** poisonous ragwort and groundsel have many yellow flowers on tall, leafy, branching stems. Pick only plants with a flower or the round white seed head.

**Prepare:** eat young leaves and flowers raw. Boil older, bitter leaves for 5 minutes and throw away the water. Boil or roast the roots.

### Cattail (bulrush, reedmace)

**Identify:** dense stands in shallow water, 1 to 3 m (3 to 10 ft) tall, with long, flat, sword-like leaves and a brown, sausage-shaped seed head on a stiff stem. Old heads stay on dead stems through winter.

**Look-alikes:** young irises, some poisonous, grow in the same places. **Harvest only where you can see brown seed heads on the same plants.**

**Prepare:** peel young shoots and cook the white core. Roots hold the most calories: crush peeled roots in water, let the starch settle, pour off the water and cook the starch like flour. Cattails absorb pollution: avoid water near sewage, mines, farms and roads.

### Plantain (Plantago)

Not the banana-like fruit. **Identify:** a flat ring of oval or narrow leaves, each with 3 to 7 strong veins running side by side from base to tip. Torn slowly, the veins pull out like strings. Flowers form a thin green-brown spike on a leafless stem.

**Look-alikes:** poisonous false hellebore, lily of the valley and young irises also have parallel veins, but no flat ring with thin flower spikes. Pick only plants that show the spikes.

**Prepare:** eat young leaves raw or cooked. Cook older, tough leaves.

### Clover

**Identify:** a low plant, never woody. Leaves have 3 rounded leaflets, often with a pale V mark. Flower heads are round balls of tiny white, pink or red flowers.

**Look-alikes:** poison ivy and poison oak have 3 larger, pointed leaflets on woody vines or shrubs. Touching them causes a bad rash.

**Prepare:** boil leaves and flowers for 5 to 10 minutes; raw clover is hard to digest. Eat small amounts. Never eat moldy clover: moldy sweet clover contains a blood-thinning poison.

### Stinging nettle

**Identify:** upright, 0.5 to 2 m (2 to 6 ft) tall, with a square stem and pointed, saw-toothed leaves in opposite pairs. Stinging hairs burn when touched.

**Look-alikes:** dead-nettles look similar but do not sting and are not dangerous. In tropical Asia and Australia, stinging trees with large heart-shaped leaves cause severe pain for weeks: do not touch them.

**Prepare:** wear gloves. Pick the top 4 to 6 leaves of young plants before they flower. Boil or steam for **at least 2 minutes** to destroy the sting. Never eat them raw. Old, flowering plants can irritate the kidneys.

### Acorns

**Identify:** the nut of oak trees, a smooth brown nut sitting in a **scaly cup**.

**Look-alikes:** poisonous horse chestnut and buckeye seeds are round and shiny with a pale scar, grow in a thick, often spiny husk, and have **no scaly cup**.

**Prepare:** acorns contain bitter tannins that harm the stomach and kidneys. Leach them:

1. Throw away acorns with holes or mold, and any that float. Shell the rest and chop the kernels finely.
2. Boil, pour off the brown water and add fresh boiling water. Repeat until the water stays almost clear, often 5 to 10 times. Or soak in cold water for 3 to 7 days, changing it 2 to 3 times a day.
3. Taste a small piece. If it is still bitter, keep leaching.
4. Cook as porridge, or dry and grind into flour.

Acorns give about **390 kcal per 100 g** (3.5 oz) fresh and 500 kcal dried, which is rare for wild food.

### Pine needle tea and inner bark

**Identify true pines:** needles grow in **bundles of 2, 3 or 5**, held together at the base by a thin papery sheath.

**Deadly look-alike: yew.** Yew needles grow **one by one**, flat, dark green on top and paler below, in flat rows. Yew has red cup-shaped berries with a dark seed. Every part except the red flesh is deadly and can stop the heart within hours. Never use a tree with single needles. Other trees called "pine" or "cedar" may also be poisonous.

> [!WARNING]
> **Do not drink pine needle tea if you are pregnant.** Some pines cause miscarriage in cattle, and the risk in people is not known.

**Tea:** chop a small handful of fresh green needles. Pour 250 mL (1 cup) of just-boiled water over them, cover and steep for 10 to 20 minutes. Do not keep boiling it: heat destroys its vitamin C.

**Inner bark:** the soft, pale, moist layer between the outer bark and the wood, best in spring. Scrape it from a peeled branch. Eat it raw, boiled, or dried and ground into flour.

### Wild berries

- **Aggregate berries are the safest group.** Blackberries and raspberries are made of many small juicy bubbles packed together, on thorny, arching canes.
- **Look-alikes:** deadly nightshade (shiny black single berries in a green star-shaped collar) and pokeweed (dark purple berries in hanging clusters on red stems) grow in the same hedges. They are smooth single berries, not clusters of bubbles.
- **Never eat white or yellow wild berries.**
- **Never eat red, blue or black berries you cannot name with certainty.** Many are poisonous, including yew, holly, ivy, privet and nightshades.

## Where you pick matters

- **Roads and railways:** exhaust and weed killers settle on plants. Pick well away, ideally 50 m (160 ft) or more.
- **Sprayed land:** field edges, parks, lawns, under power lines. Strips of yellow, twisted or dying plants are a sign of spraying.
- **Polluted ground:** below mines and factories, near sewage, and wherever floodwater has been.
- **The law:** many countries ban digging up wild plants, picking in nature reserves, or picking on private land without permission. In a true emergency, survival comes first. Take only what you need.
