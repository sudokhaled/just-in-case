Your body, the sun, sound and common objects can measure most things well enough for survival tasks. All of these methods are rough. They work much better if you check your own body measurements once, while you still have a ruler, and write them down. **Never use rough methods for medicine doses.**

## Quick steps: calibrate yourself now

1. **Measure** your thumb width, hand span, elbow-to-fingertip length and height with a ruler. No ruler? Use a bank card: it is 8.56 x 5.40 cm (3.37 x 2.13 in).
2. **Count** your double steps over a known 100 m (110 yd), such as the straight of a running track, 3 times. Use the average.
3. **Write** your numbers down and keep them with this library.

## Your body as a ruler

| Body measure | How | Typical adult | Yours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thumb width | Across the knuckle | About 2.5 cm (1 in) | |
| Hand span | Thumb tip to little finger tip, spread wide | About 20 cm (8 in) | |
| Forearm | Elbow to tip of middle finger | About 45 cm (18 in) | |
| Arm span | Fingertip to fingertip, arms spread | About your height | |
| Pace (double step) | Count each time the same foot lands | About 1.5 m (5 ft) | |

Other known sizes: A4 paper is 21.0 x 29.7 cm (8.3 x 11.7 in). US Letter paper is 21.6 x 27.9 cm (8.5 x 11 in).

## Pacing distance

- Most adults take about 60 to 70 double steps per 100 m on flat ground. Use your own count.
- Your steps get shorter on slopes, in sand, mud or snow, in the dark, with a heavy load, and when tired.
- To keep count, move a pebble from one pocket to another every 100 m, or tie a knot in a cord.

For finding your way, see [Navigating Without GPS](../navigation/navigate-no-gps.md).

## Estimating distance

### Thumb-jump method

1. Hold your arm straight out, thumb up. Close your left eye and line up your thumb with the object.
2. Keep your thumb still and switch eyes: open the left, close the right. The thumb seems to jump sideways.
3. Estimate how far it jumped, at the object, by comparing with known sizes: a car is about 4.5 m (15 ft) long.
4. **Multiply that jump by 10.** That is the distance to the object.

It works because your arm is about 10 times longer than the gap between your eyes. It is rough, because the jump is hard to judge.

### Sound delay

- **Lightning:** count the seconds from the flash to the thunder. Divide by **3** for kilometers, or by **5** for miles. 9 seconds is about 3 km (2 miles).
- If you can hear thunder at all, you are close enough to be struck. Go indoors or into a hard-topped vehicle, and wait 30 minutes after the last thunder. See [Hurricanes, Cyclones and Tornadoes](../disasters/storms.md).

## Estimating height

### Shadow method (best on flat ground)

1. Push a straight stick upright into flat ground.
2. Measure the stick's height above the ground and the length of its shadow.
3. Measure the shadow of the tree or building, from its base to the tip.
4. **Height = object's shadow x stick height / stick shadow.**

Example: a 1 m stick casts a 1.5 m shadow, and the tree's shadow is 30 m. The tree is 30 x 1 / 1.5 = **20 m (65 ft)** tall. Measure both shadows within a few minutes.

### Stick method

1. Hold a straight stick upright in your fist, with your arm straight out.
2. Adjust it so the part above your fist is as long as the distance from your eye to your fist.
3. Walk toward or away from the tree until the top of your fist lines up with its base and the stick tip lines up with its top.
4. Pace the distance to the tree. **That distance is about the height.**

A building is about 3 m (10 ft) per floor.

## Time from the sun

> [!WARNING]
> Never look straight at the sun. Glance only, or let your top finger cover it.

### Daylight left before sunset

1. Stretch your arm out straight. Hold your hand flat, fingers together and sideways, palm toward you.
2. Put the bottom edge of your little finger on the horizon.
3. Count finger widths up to the bottom of the sun. Stack your other hand on top if needed.
4. The common rule is **about 15 minutes per finger,** and 1 hour per hand of 4 fingers.

This is rough and often too generous. Near the equator, and at mid-latitudes outside summer, the sun drops faster, so one finger may mean only about 10 minutes. Far from the equator in summer it can mean more. Treat the result as the most time you have, and test it one evening while you still have a watch.

### Noon and time of day

- The sun is highest and shadows are shortest at **solar noon**, halfway between sunrise and sunset.
- Clock time often differs from solar time by 30 minutes to 1 hour or more, because of time zones and daylight saving time.
- Near the equator, the sun rises around 6:00 and sets around 18:00 solar time all year.

## Measuring volume

| Container | Volume |
|---|---|
| Standard water bottle | 500 mL (16.9 fl oz). Check the label. |
| Drink can | 330 mL in many countries, 355 mL (12 fl oz) in North America |
| Jerrycan | Often 20 liters (about 5 US gallons) |
| Measuring teaspoon | 5 mL |
| Measuring tablespoon | 15 mL |
| Measuring cup | 250 mL (US cup: 240 mL) |
| Medicine dropper | About 20 drops per 1 mL. Drop size varies. |
| US gallon / UK gallon | 3.8 liters / 4.5 liters |

To mark a bucket, pour in a full 500 mL bottle twice and scratch a line at 1 liter. Repeat for each liter. Use measured water for water treatment and oral rehydration solution. See [Making Water Safe to Drink](../water/purify-water.md) and [Dehydration, Diarrhea and Oral Rehydration](../medical/dehydration.md).

> [!WARNING]
> Spoons used for eating are not measuring spoons. Household teaspoons have been found to hold from about 2.5 to 7 mL. For medicine, use the cup or syringe that comes with it.

## Weighing with a balance

**1 liter of water weighs 1 kg (2.2 lb).** A full 500 mL bottle weighs about 0.5 kg (1.1 lb), plus a few grams for the bottle.

```
                  |   string at the exact middle
    o=============+=============o   straight stick
    |                           |
    |                           |
  [item]                     [water]
    |--- 40 cm ---|--- 40 cm ---|
```

1. Tie a string to the exact middle of a straight stick, so it hangs level.
2. Hang two identical bags or containers at the same distance from the middle.
3. Put the item on one side. Add water to the other until the stick hangs level.
4. The item weighs the same as the water: 1 liter = 1 kg.

For heavy loads, move the item closer to the middle. **Weight x distance from the middle** must be equal on both sides: 1 kg hung 40 cm out balances 2 kg hung 20 cm out.

## River speed and width

### Speed

1. Pace out 10 m (33 ft) along the bank. Mark both ends.
2. Throw a stick into the main current, upstream of the first mark.
3. Count the seconds it takes to travel between the marks.
4. **Speed in meters per second = 10 / seconds.** 10 seconds means 1 m/s (3.6 km/h, 2.2 mph).

The surface in the middle is the fastest part. The average flow is slower, roughly 0.8 times what the stick shows.

### Width

```
      A   tree on far bank
~~~~~~|~\~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
~~~~~~|~~~\~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ river
~~~~~~|~~~~~\~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
      |       \
      B---------C---------D  your bank
                  \       |
                    \     |
                      \   |
                        \ |
                          E
```

1. Pick a clear object on the far bank (A). Stand directly opposite it (B).
2. Walk along your bank at a right angle to the line B-A. Count your paces, for example 50. Push a stick in the ground (C).
3. Walk the same number of paces again in the same direction. Stop (D).
4. Turn away from the river at a right angle. Walk inland until stick C and object A line up. Stop (E).
5. **Count the paces from D to E. That is the width of the river.**

> [!CAUTION]
> Fast water is stronger than it looks. Moving water only 15 cm (6 in) deep can knock an adult over. See [Floods](../disasters/flood.md).
