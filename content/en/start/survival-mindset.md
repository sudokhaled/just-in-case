> [!CAUTION]
> **Panic makes people do deadly things:** run back into a burning building, drive into floodwater, leave a safe shelter at night, or walk away from the group. Unless you are in danger this second, stop for 1 minute and breathe before any big move.
>
> **If someone talks about wanting to die, take it seriously.** Stay with them. Remove pills, weapons and ropes. Listen without judging. See [Psychological First Aid](../community/psychological-first-aid.md).

## Quick steps

1. **Stop.** Sit or stand still. Unless there is danger right now, do nothing for 1 minute.
2. **Breathe slowly.** In for 4 seconds, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4. Repeat 4 times.
3. **Look around.** Name 5 things you can see, out loud or in your head.
4. **Choose one small next task.** Drink water, put on a coat, check on a child.
5. **Do it, then choose the next one.**

## The STOP method

Use this when you are lost, shocked, or do not know what to do.

- **S - Stop.** Do not rush. Sit down if you can. Drink and eat something if you have it.
- **T - Think.** What happened? What is the danger right now? What do I have with me?
- **O - Observe.** Look at the place, the weather, the time left before dark, the people, their injuries and your supplies.
- **P - Plan.** Choose the next step. Deal with the fastest danger first (see [Survival Priorities: What Kills First](../start/survival-priorities.md)). Then act.

Repeat STOP whenever things change.

## Calm your body first

Fear is a body reaction. Your heart races, you breathe fast, your hands shake and you cannot think clearly. This is normal. It helps you run, but it makes careful work and decisions harder. Slow breathing is the fastest way to turn it down, because a long out-breath slows the heart.

### Box breathing

1. Breathe in through your nose for 4 seconds.
2. Hold for 4 seconds.
3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for 4 seconds.
4. Hold for 4 seconds.
5. Repeat for 1 to 5 minutes.

If holding your breath feels bad, skip the holds. Just make each out-breath longer than the in-breath.

### Double in-breath, long out-breath

Breathe in deeply through your nose. Then take a second short breath in on top of it. Then breathe out slowly through your mouth until your lungs are empty. Repeat for up to 5 minutes. In a 2023 study, 5 minutes a day of this "cyclic sighing" improved mood more than mindfulness meditation did.

### Panic attacks

Signs: racing heart, tight chest, short breath, dizziness, tingling hands, shaking, and a strong feeling that you are dying or going mad.

- A panic attack is very frightening but not dangerous in itself. It usually passes in 5 to 20 minutes.
- Breathe slowly, with long out-breaths. Do **not** breathe into a paper bag.
- Press your feet into the ground. Name 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell and 1 you taste.
- Tell yourself: "This is panic. It will pass."

> [!WARNING]
> Not every racing heart is panic. Chest pain with sweating, or pain that spreads to the arm or jaw, may be a heart attack, especially in older people. If several people indoors feel headache, dizziness or sickness at the same time, it may be carbon monoxide: get everyone outside now. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).

**To help someone in panic:** stay with them. Use a calm, slow voice and short sentences. Breathe slowly with them and ask them to copy you. Do not say "calm down". Say "I am here. Breathe with me."

## Routines

Routines give back a feeling of control and save energy for real problems.

- Set fixed times for waking, meals, water collection, chores, checking supplies and sleep.
- Give every person a job, including children and old people.
- Each morning, write 3 tasks for the day. Cross them off when done.
- Wash, change clothes and brush teeth when you can. Small normal acts help people feel human.
- Keep children's routines: meals, play, stories, songs and simple lessons.
- Listen to news and rumors at set times, for example twice a day. Constant bad news raises stress and helps nobody.

## Sleep

Lack of sleep causes mistakes, accidents and bad moods. After 17 to 19 hours awake, people perform about as badly as someone with a blood alcohol level of 0.05%, which is over the driving limit in many countries.

- Adults need about 7 hours or more. Children and teenagers need more.
- If someone must stay awake on watch, take turns in shifts of 2 to 4 hours so everyone sleeps.
- Make sleep possible: stay warm and dry, put insulation under you, cover your eyes, and plug your ears with soft cloth.
- Short naps of 10 to 20 minutes improve alertness without leaving you groggy.
- Avoid tea, coffee and other caffeine in the hours before sleep. Avoid alcohol.
- If you cannot sleep, lie still with your eyes closed. Rest still helps.
- Do not do dangerous work, such as driving, using a chainsaw or climbing a roof, when you are exhausted.

## Making decisions under stress

Tired, hungry, frightened people make worse decisions. Scientists still argue about exactly why, but the effect is real. Protect your best thinking:

- **Decide important things when rested, fed and calm,** often in the morning.
- **Make rules in advance,** so you do not decide again each time: "All water gets treated." "The generator stays outside." "Nobody goes out alone." "Everyone is back before dark."
- **Write it down:** plans, lists, who does what.
- **Reduce small choices:** fixed jobs, fixed meal times, the same simple meals.
- **One leader per task.** Agree as a group how decisions are made.
- **Before a big decision, ask:** what is the worst that can happen? Can it be undone? If it cannot be undone, slow down and talk it through.

## The will to live

Survival manuals agree: the will to keep going matters as much as skills and equipment.

- Find your reason: your children, your family, people waiting for you, your faith, a promise.
- Think in short pieces of time: get through the next hour, then the next night.
- Notice small wins: the fire is lit, there is water, a child laughed.
- Focus on what you can control, and let go of what you cannot.
- Expect bad moments: grief, crying, anger, numbness. They are normal reactions to an abnormal event, and they pass.
- Do not use alcohol or drugs to cope. They make fear, sleep and decisions worse.

## Keeping other people going

Research after disasters shows that people recover best when they feel 5 things: **safe, calm, able to act, connected to others, and hopeful.**

- **Safe:** find shelter, warmth, food and water. Tell people what is being done to protect them.
- **Calm:** speak slowly and quietly. Keep children near calm adults.
- **Able to act:** give everyone useful work, even small jobs. Being useful helps more than being looked after.
- **Connected:** keep families together. Nobody sleeps or works alone. Eat together. Help people contact their relatives.
- **Hopeful:** talk about the plan and the next steps. Celebrate progress.
- **Be honest.** Say what you know and what you do not know. Rumors cause more fear than the truth.
- **Listen, but do not push.** Let people talk if they want to. Do not force anyone to describe what happened.
- **Respect beliefs.** Prayer, songs, stories and rituals help many people.

Watch for people who need more help: someone who stops eating or drinking, does not move or speak, is very confused, cannot care for their children, or talks about suicide. Stay with them and get help if any exists. See [Keeping Children Safe and Calm](../community/children-safety.md) for children.

Skills, health and friends are worth more than supplies in a long crisis. See [What Is Worth the Most When Everything Stops](../reference/most-valuable.md).
