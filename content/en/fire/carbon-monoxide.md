> [!CAUTION]
> **Never use a generator, charcoal, barbecue grill, camp stove or patio heater indoors,** or in a garage, basement, shed, tent or car, even with doors and windows open.
>
> **If several people or pets feel headache, dizziness or sickness at the same time indoors, get everyone outside into fresh air now.** Do not stop to look for the cause.
>
> **Carbon monoxide (CO) has no color, smell or taste.** You cannot sense it. People die in their sleep without waking up.

## Quick steps

If you think someone has carbon monoxide poisoning:

1. **Get everyone out into fresh air now,** including pets. Leave doors open behind you.
2. **Rescue only if you can do it fast.** If someone is unconscious inside, open the doors wide, take a deep breath, go in and drag them out. Do not stay inside. Rescuers die too.
3. **Check breathing.** If they are not breathing normally, start CPR. See [CPR: When Someone Is Not Breathing](../medical/cpr.md). If they are unconscious but breathing, roll them onto their side.
4. **Get medical help** if any exists. Say that you suspect carbon monoxide. Hospitals treat it with oxygen.
5. **Keep them resting, warm and in fresh air.** Give oxygen if you have it.
6. **Do not go back in** until the source is off and the building has been fully aired out. If you have a CO alarm, it must be silent.

## What makes carbon monoxide

Anything that burns fuel makes CO: gasoline (petrol), diesel, propane, natural gas, kerosene (paraffin), charcoal, wood and coal. Most deaths after storms and power cuts come from generators and charcoal used indoors for power or heat.

| Source | Rule |
|---|---|
| Portable generator | Outside only, at least **6 m (20 ft)** from doors, windows and vents, with the exhaust pointing away from buildings. Never in a garage, basement, shed, porch or crawlspace, even with the door open |
| Charcoal grill, barbecue, brazier | Outside only. Never bring it inside to heat a room, even after the flames die: glowing coals make the most CO |
| Camp stove, gas cartridge stove, patio heater | Outside, unless the label says it is safe indoors. Never in a tent |
| Car, truck, motorbike | Never run the engine in a garage, even with the door open |
| Car stuck in snow | Clear snow from the exhaust pipe. Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour. Open a window on the side away from the wind a little |
| Gas oven or stovetop | For cooking only, with a window open. **Never** use it to heat the home |
| Indoor gas or kerosene heater | Only models made for indoor use. Follow the label. Open a window a little. Turn it off before sleeping. Refuel outside, when it is cold |
| Wood stove, fireplace, boiler, gas water heater | Keep chimneys, flues and vents clear. Check them after storms, heavy snow and earthquakes |

For safer ways to stay warm indoors, see [Keeping a Home Warm Without Power](../shelter/heat-home-no-power.md).

## Signs of carbon monoxide poisoning

**Early signs:** headache (the most common), dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain, confusion, sleepiness. It feels like flu or food poisoning, but there is no fever.

**Clues that it is CO:**

- More than one person, or a pet, is sick at the same time.
- People feel better outside and worse when they go back in.
- The illness started after a generator, heater, stove or grill was turned on.

**Severe signs:** fainting, collapse, seizures, coma, death.

- People who are asleep or drunk can die before they notice anything.
- Unborn babies, babies, old people and people with heart or lung disease are at highest risk.
- Bright "cherry-red" skin is rare and comes late. Do not wait for it.

## Care when there is no hospital

- **Fresh air is the treatment.** In fresh air, the body clears half of the CO in about 4 to 6 hours. Pure oxygen from a mask cuts this to about 1 to 1.5 hours.
- Keep the person still and warm. Movement uses oxygen.
- If they are drowsy or vomiting, lay them on their side and stay with them. Be ready to start CPR.
- Do not let anyone sleep in the place where it happened until the cause is found and fixed.
- **Even after people recover,** problems with memory, mood, movement or headaches can appear days to weeks later. Anyone who fainted, was confused, or is pregnant should see a health worker as soon as one is available.

## Using a generator safely

1. Put it outside on dry ground, under an open shelter if it is raining, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents. Farther is better.
2. Point the exhaust away from your home and your neighbors' homes.
3. Run heavy outdoor extension cords to the appliances.
4. **Never plug a generator into a wall socket.** Power can flow back into the lines and kill neighbors or repair workers.
5. Turn it off and let it cool before refueling. Fuel spilled on a hot engine can catch fire.
6. Keep a battery CO alarm inside the home.

For more on generators and power, see [Long Power Outages](../disasters/power-outage.md).

## CO alarms

- Use alarms that run on batteries or have battery backup, so they work in power cuts.
- Put one on every floor and near every sleeping area.
- Press the test button every month. Change the batteries twice a year.
- Replace the alarm by the date printed on it. Most last 5 to 10 years.
- Smoke alarms do not detect CO, unless they are marked as combined alarms.
- **If the alarm sounds, get everyone outside first.** Do not just switch it off and stay inside.
- An alarm is a backup. It does not make it safe to burn charcoal or run a generator indoors.

## Smoke from cooking and heating fires

Smoke contains tiny particles, CO and other poisons. Breathing it every day from cooking fires causes pneumonia in children, lung disease, lung cancer, heart disease and stroke. The World Health Organization estimates that smoke from household cooking kills about 3 million people every year.

To cut the smoke:

- **Cook outside,** or under a roof with open sides, whenever you can.
- **Burn only dry wood.** Wet wood makes much more smoke. Split it and dry it under cover.
- **Keep the fire small and hot.** A smoldering fire makes more smoke than a bright one.
- **Use a stove with a chimney or a rocket stove.** See [Cooking Without Power](../fire/cook-without-power.md).
- **Let air through.** Open windows or doors on opposite sides of the room. Make a smoke hole or hood above an indoor fire.
- **Keep babies and children away from the smoke.** Do not carry a baby on your back while you cook over a smoky fire.
- **Spend less time in the smoke.** Prepare food away from the fire. Use lids so food cooks faster.
- **Never burn plastic, rubber, painted or treated wood, or rubbish** indoors or near food.
- Kerosene lamps and candles also make smoke and fumes. Give them fresh air.

In a burning building, smoke kills more people than flames do. Stay low, under the smoke, and close doors behind you as you leave. See [Wildfires and House Fires](../disasters/wildfire.md).
