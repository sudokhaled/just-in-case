> [!CAUTION]
> **Never short-circuit a phone, laptop, power bank or car battery to make sparks.** Lithium batteries can burst into flames, and car batteries can explode.
>
> **Store steel wool and batteries apart.** Steel wool that touches a battery in a pocket or bag can catch fire.
>
> **Build the whole fire before you make a spark or ember.** An ember dies within 1 or 2 minutes.

## Quick steps

1. **Prepare everything:** a tinder bundle, kindling in 3 sizes, and fuel. See [Making and Keeping a Fire](../fire/make-fire.md).
2. **Choose the easiest method you have** (see the table).
3. **Catch the spark or ember** in very fine, dry tinder.
4. **Blow gently** until it bursts into flame.
5. **Put it under the kindling at once.**

| Method | Difficulty | Works in wet weather? | You need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Empty lighter | Easy | If kept dry | Char cloth or very fine tinder |
| Ferro rod | Easy | Yes, wipe it dry | Fine, fluffy tinder |
| Battery and steel wool | Easy | If kept dry | 9-volt battery, fine steel wool |
| Lens and sun | Medium | No, needs strong sun | Lens, dark tinder |
| Flint and steel | Medium | If char cloth is dry | Carbon steel, hard stone, char cloth |
| Bow drill | Hard | No | The right dry wood, cord, practice |

## The tinder bundle

Make a loose ball the size of your fist from fine, dry material: coarser outside (dry grass, shredded bark), finest inside (shredded inner bark, plant fluff, cotton). Press a hollow into the middle.

1. Put the ember in the hollow and fold the bundle gently around it.
2. Hold it up and blow into it with long, gentle breaths.
3. When it bursts into flame, put it straight under the kindling.

Cotton balls rubbed with petroleum jelly burn for a few minutes each. Dry rotten wood that crumbles in your fingers holds a spark well.

## Empty lighter

A disposable lighter with no fuel still makes sparks. Hold it close to char cloth or fine fluffy tinder and spin the wheel hard. Dry a wet wheel on dry cloth first.

## Ferro rod

A ferrocerium (ferro) rod throws very hot sparks, even after it has been wet. On a new rod, scrape off the black coating first.

1. Put the tip of the rod into the tinder.
2. Press the striker, or the square back of a knife, firmly against the rod at a steep angle.
3. **Pull the rod back toward you** while holding the striker still. This throws sparks without scattering the tinder.
4. Repeat with slow, firm strokes until the tinder catches.

**Magnesium block:** scrape a coin-sized pile of shavings onto the tinder, then throw sparks into it. It burns very hot for a few seconds.

## Battery and steel wool

1. Fluff out a piece of the finest steel wool (grade 0000 or 000), about the size of an egg.
2. Touch both terminals of a 9-volt battery to it at the same time.
3. It glows and spreads at once. Put it into the tinder and blow gently.

Smaller 1.5-volt batteries (AA, C, D) work less well. Put 2 end to end and stretch a thin strip of steel wool from the top of one to the bottom of the other.

## Sun and lens

This works only in strong, direct sun, around the middle of the day.

1. **Use a lens that makes things look bigger:** a magnifying glass, a lens from binoculars or a camera, or reading glasses. Glasses for nearsighted people make things look smaller and **do not work.**
2. **Use dark tinder:** char cloth, charred wood or dark rotten wood.
3. Move the lens until the bright spot on the tinder is as small as possible.
4. Hold it very still. The tinder smokes within seconds to a minute. Blow gently, then move it into the bundle.

A clear, round bottle full of clear water is a weak lens that focuses only a few centimeters (inches) away. It is hard to make work. Never look at the sun through any lens.

## Flint and steel with char cloth

- **Steel:** high-carbon steel, such as an old file, the back of a carbon steel knife or a fire striker. Stainless steel works poorly.
- **Stone:** a hard stone with a sharp edge, such as flint, chert or quartz. If it scratches glass, it is hard enough.

1. Hold the stone sharp edge up. Hold a piece of char cloth on top, just behind the edge, under your thumb.
2. Strike the steel down along the edge with fast, glancing blows.
3. When a tiny glowing spot appears on the cloth, blow gently to spread it.
4. Fold the cloth into the tinder bundle and blow it into flame.

## Making char cloth

1. Cut 100% cotton cloth (old jeans, a cotton t-shirt) into 5 cm (2 in) squares. Synthetic cloth does not work.
2. Put them loosely into a small metal tin with a tight lid. Make 1 small hole in the lid with a nail.
3. Put the tin on hot coals. Smoke and gas come out of the hole, and may burn as a small flame.
4. When the smoke stops, often after 5 to 15 minutes, take the tin out.
5. Plug the hole with dirt and let the tin cool completely before opening it, or the cloth burns up in the air.

Good char cloth is black and bends without breaking. Brown means undercooked. Crumbly means overcooked. Keep it dry in the tin.

## Bow drill

Be honest with yourself: most beginners need many tries over hours or days to make their first ember. **Practice at home before you need it.**

| Part | Size | Material |
|---|---|---|
| Spindle | 2 cm (3/4 in) thick, 20-25 cm (8-10 in) long, straight | Dry, dead, soft wood |
| Fireboard | 2 cm (3/4 in) thick, 5-8 cm (2-3 in) wide, 30 cm (12 in) long | The same wood as the spindle |
| Bow | About the length of your arm, stiff, slightly curved | Any strong stick, with a bootlace or strong cord |
| Handhold | Fits your palm | Hard wood, a stone with a dip, bone or shell |

**Woods that often work:** willow, poplar, aspen, cottonwood, basswood (lime, linden), cedar, juniper. Use dead, dry wood that is not rotten and dents under your thumbnail. Avoid hard wood such as oak, and resinous pine.

1. **Shape the spindle:** pointed at the top, blunt and rounded at the bottom.
2. **Make the handhold:** carve a small hollow for the spindle top and grease it with soap, fat or crushed green leaves. **Never** grease the bottom of the spindle.
3. **Start the hole:** cut a small dent in the board, so the spindle sits about 5 mm (1/4 in) in from the edge.
4. **Get into position** (if right-handed): left foot on the board beside the dent, right knee on the ground.
5. **Load the spindle:** twist it once into the bow cord, cord on the outside. It must grip tightly.
6. **Brace it:** spindle in the dent, handhold on top in your left hand. Lock your left wrist against your left shin.
7. **Burn in the hole:** saw the bow with long, slow strokes and light pressure until the hole is black and as wide as the spindle.
8. **Cut the notch:** a V-shaped slice from the board's edge to the center of the hole, about 1/8 of the circle. Put thin bark or a leaf under it to catch the ember.
9. **Drill:** long, full strokes with steady downward pressure. Speed up when it smokes. Keep going for 10 to 20 strokes after heavy smoke appears and dark powder fills the notch.
10. **Lift the spindle away carefully.** If the powder keeps smoking on its own, it is an ember. Fan it gently until it glows, then tip it into the tinder bundle.

**If it fails:**

- **Spindle jumps out:** brace your wrist harder, deepen the handhold hollow, or tighten the cord.
- **Hole turns shiny with little smoke:** the wood is too hard or resinous. Try other wood.
- **Powder is light brown and coarse:** go faster and press harder, or the wood is damp.
- **You are exhausted:** take turns with a partner.

## Practice before you need it

- Try every method on a dry day, before an emergency.
- Keep 3 ways to make fire in your emergency kit, for example a lighter, matches in a waterproof container and a ferro rod. See [Emergency Kit Checklists](../reference/emergency-kits.md).
- Keep dry tinder in a sealed bag, and a tin of char cloth.

If you are wet and cold and cannot make fire, get out of the wind and insulate yourself from the ground. See [Staying Warm in the Cold](../shelter/stay-warm.md).
