> [!CAUTION]
> **Never burn a fire, charcoal or stove inside a tent, car or any room without a working chimney.** Smoke and carbon monoxide kill silently. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).
>
> **Never pour gasoline (petrol), alcohol or other liquid fuel onto a fire,** not even a small one or one that seems to be out. The vapor explodes and causes severe burns.
>
> **Never leave a fire alone, and put it out until it is cold to the touch.** Buried coals can stay hot for hours and start a wildfire.

## Quick steps

1. **Choose a safe spot:** bare soil, at least 5 m (15 ft) from tents, trees and bushes, not under branches. Clear a circle 3 m (10 ft) across down to bare soil.
2. **Gather everything before you light it:** 2 big handfuls of tinder, an armload of kindling, and a pile of fuel wood. Gather twice what you think you need.
3. **Build a small teepee:** tinder in the center, the thinnest kindling leaned around it, with a gap on the side the wind comes from.
4. **Kneel with your back to the wind** and light the bottom of the tinder.
5. **Feed it slowly:** thin sticks first, then thicker ones. Keep gaps for air.
6. **Add fuel wood** only when the kindling burns strongly.
7. **To put it out:** drown it, stir it, drown it again, and feel for heat.

## How fire works

A fire needs **heat, fuel and air.** Take away any one and it dies. Most fires fail because the wood is too thick or too wet, so there is not enough heat, or because it is packed too tightly, so there is not enough air.

## Choose a safe site

- Build on bare dirt, sand or gravel. Do not build on peat, deep dead leaves or tree roots: fire can creep underground and come up later, far away.
- Keep at least 5 m (15 ft) from tents, shelters, dry grass, bushes and trees. Look up for low branches.
- Choose a place sheltered from strong wind. In strong wind and dry weather, do not light an open fire at all.
- If you make a ring of stones, use dry stones. Wet stones from rivers can crack or burst when heated.
- On snow or wet ground, build on a platform of thick green (living) sticks laid side by side, or on flat dry stones.
- Keep water, sand or dirt, and a stick or shovel, beside the fire.
- In dry seasons many places ban open fires, because campfires start many wildfires. Follow these rules unless your life depends on a fire.

For warmth, build a wall of logs or rocks behind the fire. It reflects heat back toward you. See [Staying Warm in the Cold](../shelter/stay-warm.md).

## Tinder, kindling and fuel

| Type | Size | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Tinder | Fine as hair or paper | Dry grass, birch bark, shredded inner bark, dry pine needles, wood shavings, cattail fluff, paper, cotton balls with petroleum jelly, dryer lint |
| Kindling | Matchstick to thumb thick | Dead, dry twigs that snap with a crack; split wood; resin-rich pine wood |
| Fuel | Wrist thick and larger | Dead branches, split logs |

- **Everything must be dry.** If a stick bends instead of snapping, it is too wet or still alive.
- Sort kindling into 3 piles: matchstick, pencil and thumb thick.
- Dead branches still on trees, and dead standing trees, are drier than wood lying on the ground. For how much wood a winter takes and how to dry and store it, see [Living in a Forest](../land/forest-living.md).
- The inside of dead wood is often dry. Split it.
- **Feather stick:** shave thin curls along a dry stick, leaving them attached. The curls catch fire easily.
- Birch bark burns even when damp, because it contains oils. Pine resin (sap) burns hot and long.
- Hardwoods such as oak, beech, maple and ash burn longer and make good coals. Softwoods such as pine, spruce and fir light fast, burn fast and spark more.
- **Never burn** plastic, rubber, painted or treated wood, or poison ivy, poison oak or poison sumac. The smoke is toxic, and smoke from these plants can badly damage the lungs and eyes.

## Fire lays

### Teepee (tipi)

Put the tinder in the middle. Lean the thinnest kindling around it in a cone. Leave a small gap on the side the wind comes from, to light through. Lean thicker sticks on as it grows. It burns hot and fast, so it is good for starting a fire and for boiling water.

### Log cabin

Build a small teepee. Around it, stack sticks in a square: 2 parallel sticks, then 2 across them, like a small house, 4 to 6 layers high. It burns steadily and makes a good bed of coals for cooking. To cook with much less wood, see [Cooking Without Power](../fire/cook-without-power.md).

### Lean-to (for wind)

Lay a thick log or a rock on the side the wind comes from. Put the tinder against the sheltered side. Lean kindling over the tinder, resting on the log. The log shields the flame from the wind.

### Star fire (saves fuel)

Lay 4 to 6 logs on the ground, pointing to the center like the spokes of a wheel. Light a fire where the ends meet. As the ends burn, push the logs inward. It uses little wood and is easy to make bigger or smaller.

## Lighting the fire

1. Have everything ready. The next size of kindling must be within reach.
2. Kneel with your back to the wind, so your body shields the flame.
3. Light the tinder at the bottom. Flames climb upward.
4. Hold a match with the head pointing down, so the flame climbs the stick. Cup your hands around it.
5. When the tinder smokes or flickers, blow gently at the base with long, slow breaths.
6. Add kindling a few pieces at a time, thinnest first.
7. Add thicker sticks only when flames reach above the kindling.

A candle stub keeps a flame going for many minutes while damp kindling catches. Blow it out and save it afterward.

### In wind

- Make a windbreak with rocks, logs, a bank of earth or your body.
- Dig a shallow pit for the fire.
- Use the lean-to lay.
- Keep the fire small, and watch for sparks blowing toward dry grass or shelters.

### In rain or snow

- Look for dry tinder and kindling under thick evergreen trees, among the tiny dead twigs near the trunk, inside hollow logs, under rock overhangs and in the center of split dead wood.
- Carry your own tinder in a sealed bag: cotton balls with petroleum jelly, birch bark, resin-rich wood, paper.
- Make feather sticks from split wood.
- Build on a platform off the wet ground.
- Prepare twice as much kindling as usual. Make the fire bigger and hotter at first.
- Put wet wood near the fire, not in it, so it dries for later.
- If you hang a tarp for shelter, keep it high and to the side, never right above the flames.

## Keeping it going

- Add fuel before the flames die down. A fire is much easier to keep than to restart.
- Add a few pieces at a time. Keep air spaces between them.
- Keep a pile of dry fuel close enough to reach but safe from sparks. Cover it from rain.
- Rake the coals into the center. Push the ends of logs into the coals.
- Make one person responsible for the fire at all times.

## Banking coals overnight

Do this only in a cleared, safe site with no wind and no dry grass nearby.

1. Before you sleep, let the fire burn down to a bed of glowing coals.
2. Rake the coals into a tight pile.
3. Lay 1 or 2 thick pieces of hardwood against the pile.
4. Cover the pile with a thick layer of ash. The ash keeps the heat in and slows the air, so the coals smolder instead of burning.
5. In the morning, brush the ash away until you find glowing coals.
6. Put tinder and thin kindling on the coals and blow gently.

Banked coals often stay alive until morning. If they do not, use fine dry tinder and patience: even a few warm coals can restart a fire.

Do not try to bank a wood stove indoors by closing its air supply tightly. A smoldering stove makes much more carbon monoxide. See [Keeping a Home Warm Without Power](../shelter/heat-home-no-power.md).

## Putting it out completely

1. Let the fire burn down to ash if you can.
2. **Drown it.** Pour lots of water over all the coals until the hissing stops.
3. **Stir it.** Mix the ash and coals with a stick or shovel. Turn over logs and scrape them.
4. **Drown it again.**
5. **Feel it.** Hold the back of your hand close to the ash. If you feel no heat, touch it. If it is too hot to touch, it is too hot to leave.

With no water, mix in sand or dirt and stir until everything is cold. Do not just bury the fire: buried coals can smolder for hours and spread through roots.

## Common mistakes

- Not enough tinder and kindling ready before lighting.
- Adding thick wood too early, which smothers the fire.
- Packing wood too tightly, so no air gets in.
- Building too close to a shelter, or under branches.
- Leaving the fire "just for a minute".

If clothes catch fire, stop, drop to the ground and roll. Cool any burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. See [Burns](../medical/burns.md). If you have no matches or lighter, see [Fire Without Matches or a Lighter](../fire/fire-without-matches.md).
