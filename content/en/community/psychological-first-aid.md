> [!CAUTION]
> **If someone may kill themselves, ask them directly and do not leave them alone.** Asking "Are you thinking of killing yourself?" does not put the idea in their head. Remove anything they could use: medicines, pesticides, ropes, blades, guns.
>
> **Do not force people to talk about what happened.** Pushing someone to describe a shocking event in detail, soon after it, can make them worse. Let them talk only if they want to, and only as much as they want.
>
> **Sudden confusion may be a medical problem, not "just shock".** Check for head injury, low blood sugar, dehydration, heat stroke, infection or poisoning.

## Quick steps

1. **Look.** Make sure the place is safe. Deal with injuries and urgent needs first: bleeding, cold, thirst, lost children.
2. **Approach.** Say your name. Say you are there to help. Ask if you can sit with them.
3. **Listen.** Ask what they need and what worries them most. Listen more than you talk. Allow silence.
4. **Calm.** Speak slowly and softly. Help them feel their feet on the ground and breathe slowly.
5. **Link.** Help them get water, food, shelter, medical care, news of family, and people they trust.
6. **Get more help** for anyone who talks of suicide, may hurt someone, cannot care for themselves or their children, or sees and hears things others do not.

## What psychological first aid is

Psychological first aid (PFA) is calm, practical support for people who have just lived through something terrible. Anyone can give it: a neighbor, a teacher, a teenager. It is not therapy. You help people feel safe, calm, connected and able to help themselves.

## Look, Listen, Link

### Look

- Is it safe to stay here? If not, move the person, or do not go in.
- Who has urgent needs: injured people, separated children, older or disabled people alone? For injuries, see [First Aid Basics: Check, Call, Care](../medical/first-aid-basics.md).
- Who is in severe distress: not moving, not speaking, very confused, panicking?

### Listen

- Sit or crouch at the person's eye level. Keep a respectful distance. Ask before you touch anyone.
- Ask simple questions: "What do you need right now?" "What is worrying you most?"
- For someone panicking, say: "I am here with you. You are safe right now. Feel your feet on the floor. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly." Ask them to name 3 things they can see.

### Link

- Help with practical needs first: water, food, a blanket, a phone call, a place to sleep.
- Give true information about what is happening and where to get help. If you do not know, say: "I don't know, but I will try to find out."
- Help them find family, friends, and religious or community groups.
- Ask what helped them through hard times before. Encourage them to use those strengths.

## What to say and what not to say

| Say or do | Do not say or do |
|---|---|
| "I'm so sorry this happened to you." | "I know how you feel." |
| "Many people feel like this after something so terrible." | "You should be grateful you survived." |
| "What would help you most right now?" | "Tell me exactly what happened." |
| "I don't know, but I'll try to find out." | Guess, or make promises you cannot keep. |
| Let them make their own choices. | Take over and do everything for them. |
| Keep what they tell you private. | Repeat their story to others. |

Never ask for money, favors or sex in return for help.

> [!NOTE]
> WHO advises against "psychological debriefing", where people are made to describe the event and their feelings in detail soon after it. It does not prevent later problems and may make some people worse.

## Normal reactions to a terrible event

These are common and normal in the first days and weeks:

- Shaking, headaches, stomachache, deep tiredness, no appetite.
- Crying, sadness, fear, jumping at sudden noises, feeling always on guard.
- Trouble sleeping, nightmares, going over the event again and again.
- Anger, irritability, guilt about surviving or about not saving others.
- Feeling numb, unreal, or "in a daze".

For most people they fade over days to weeks, especially when they are safe, fed and with people they trust. Tell people this. It helps them to know they are not "going crazy".

## Signs someone needs more help

Find a health worker as soon as you can, and keep the person safe until then, if they:

- Talk about killing themselves, or have tried to hurt themselves.
- May hurt someone else.
- Cannot care for themselves or their children: not eating, not drinking, not feeding the baby.
- Cannot move, speak or respond for hours.
- Hear voices or see things others do not, or firmly believe things that are clearly untrue (for example, that everyone is poisoning them).
- Still have strong reactions after about 1 month that stop them living their daily life, or are getting worse.
- Are drinking alcohol or using drugs heavily. See [Withdrawal and Running Out of Medicines](../medical/withdrawal.md).

With someone who seems to have lost touch with reality: stay calm, keep the area quiet, and speak simply. Do not argue about their beliefs, and do not pretend to agree. Keep them away from fire, roads and water.

> [!WARNING]
> WHO advises against sleeping pills and tranquilizers (benzodiazepines) for stress or poor sleep in the first month after a shocking event. They do not prevent later problems and can cause dependence.

## If someone may be suicidal

1. **Ask directly:** "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" Use plain words.
2. **Ask about a plan:** "Have you thought about how? Do you have what you would use?" A person with a plan, the means and a time is in immediate danger.
3. **Stay with them.** Do not leave them alone, not even for a minute. Take turns with people they trust.
4. **Remove the means.** Take away medicines, pesticides and other poisons, ropes, blades, guns and fuel. Lock them up or give them to someone else to keep.
5. **Listen without judging.** Do not argue or lecture. Say: "I'm glad you told me. I want to help you stay safe."
6. **Get help.** Take them to a health worker or trained counselor as soon as you can. Check on them every day afterwards.

## Grief

Grief has no right way and no timetable. Culture and faith shape how people mourn.

- Help with practical tasks: food, childcare, arranging a burial or ceremony.
- Support the family's mourning customs as far as safety allows.
- Use the name of the person who died. Say "I'm sorry for your loss."
- Do not say "It was meant to be", "At least..." or "Be strong".
- Help families of missing people register them with the Red Cross or Red Crescent, or with the authorities.

Grief that does not ease at all after many months, and stops a person from living their life, needs extra help.

## Helping children

- Tell them often that they are safe (if true) and that it is not their fault.
- Use clear words like "died", not "went to sleep", which can make a child afraid of sleeping.
- Expect clinging, bedwetting and nightmares. Be patient. Do not punish.

See [Keeping Children Safe and Calm](../community/children-safety.md).

## Look after yourself as a helper

- Eat, drink, rest and sleep. Take short breaks.
- Work in pairs and check on each other.
- Avoid using alcohol, tobacco or drugs to cope.
- Talk with someone you trust about what you have seen.
- Accept that you cannot solve every problem. Notice what you did manage to do.
