> [!CAUTION]
> **Never touch, move or pick up an unknown object.** Mines and unexploded shells, bombs, grenades and cluster bomblets kill and maim for years after fighting stops. They can look like cans, toys, stones or scrap metal. Children are hurt most often.
>
> **When shelling or an air raid starts, get down and get behind walls at once.** Most injuries come from flying metal fragments and glass. Seconds matter.
>
> **Do not go to look at a place that was just hit, and do not film it.** A second strike on the same place can happen.

## Quick steps

1. **On a warning** (siren, phone alert, explosions), go to the nearest shelter: a basement, an underground station, or an inside room with **at least 2 walls** between you and the outside.
2. **Caught outside:** lie face down at once in a ditch, a hollow or at the foot of a solid wall. Cover your head with your hands.
3. **Stay in shelter** until the all-clear, or until well after the last explosion.
4. **Never touch unknown objects.** Walk only on paths that many people use every day.
5. **Carry your identity documents** at all times, and keep copies elsewhere.
6. **At checkpoints:** slow down, stay calm, keep your hands visible, follow instructions, take no photos.
7. **If separated from family,** go to the Red Cross or Red Crescent to trace them.

## Shelter during shelling and airstrikes

| Protection | Place |
|---|---|
| Best | Basement, cellar, underground car park, metro station, purpose-built shelter |
| Good | A corridor or room in the middle of a building, with at least 2 walls between you and the outside |
| Poor | Rooms with windows, top floors, near glass, cars, tents |

- Use the **2-wall rule:** the first wall takes the blast, the second stops the fragments. Inner corridors and windowless bathrooms often meet it.
- In tall buildings, avoid the top 2 floors. Stay away from windows. Safety film on windows reduces flying glass. Tape helps very little.
- Lie on the floor, or sit low against an inner wall. In a basement, know 2 ways out.

### If you are caught outside

1. **Drop to the ground at once,** face down. Use any hollow, ditch, curb, or the foot of a solid wall for cover.
2. **Cover your head and neck** with your hands and arms.
3. Many civil protection services teach covering your ears and keeping your mouth slightly open during explosions, to reduce pressure injury to the ears and lungs. There is little research on this, but it costs nothing.
4. Do not run across open ground while explosions continue. Move to better cover only during a pause, and only if it is close.
5. Stay down until the explosions have stopped for several minutes, or until the all-clear.
6. In a car: stop, get out, and lie down away from vehicles.

### Prepare a safe room

- Choose a basement or inside room. Remove glass objects and shelves that could fall.
- Keep there: water, food that needs no cooking, a flashlight, a battery radio, a power bank, a first aid kit with a tourniquet, medicines, blankets, a bucket toilet, a whistle, a crowbar and documents. See [Emergency Kit Checklists](../reference/emergency-kits.md) and [Stopping Severe Bleeding](../medical/severe-bleeding.md).
- When fighting comes close, fill containers with water. Turn off the gas when you go to shelter.
- Learn the local warning signals. They differ between countries and can change during a conflict.

## Mines and unexploded ordnance

**Where they are found:** former front lines, abandoned military positions and vehicles, empty houses, roadsides, overgrown fields, forests, bridges, water points, and anywhere that was shelled.

**Warning signs:** a red triangle sign with a skull or the word "MINES", or local marks such as crossed sticks, piles of stones or knotted grass. Ask local people and mine action teams which areas are safe.

### Rules

- **Never touch, move, kick or throw anything at an unknown object.** Do not collect souvenirs or scrap metal.
- Stay on hard roads and on paths that many people use every day. No shortcuts.
- Never touch, pull or cut a thin wire across a path. Do not light fires on unknown ground.
- Teach children: "Do not touch. Walk away. Tell an adult."

### If you see a suspicious object

1. **Stop.** Do not go closer.
2. Remember the exact place, using landmarks.
3. **Leave the way you came.**
4. If you can without going closer, mark the place, for example with a cloth tied to a branch.
5. Warn others and keep them away.
6. Report it to the police, military, local authorities or a mine action organization.

### If you realize you are in a minefield

1. **Stop at once. Do not move your feet.**
2. Call for help by shouting or by phone, and wait for trained help if it can come.
3. If no help can come, go back exactly in your own footprints, one step at a time.
4. **Do not run in to help someone hurt by a mine.** You may step on another one. Call for trained help, and tell the injured person to stay still.

## Checkpoints

- Approach slowly. At night, dip your headlights and switch on the light inside the car.
- Stop when told. Keep your hands visible. Stay calm and polite. Give short, true answers. No sudden movements.
- Say what you are doing before you reach for anything: "My papers are in my bag."
- **Do not take photos or film** near checkpoints, soldiers or military sites. Phones may be searched, and such photos can put you in danger.
- Do not wear military-style clothing. Travel in daylight. Tell someone your route.

## Documents

- Keep identity cards, passports, birth certificates, property papers and medical records in a waterproof bag you can grab fast. Carry your identity card on your body.
- Make copies and photos. Leave copies with a relative in another place. Write important phone numbers on paper.
- Give each child a card with their name, parents' names and phone numbers, sewn into clothing or in a pocket. Keep a recent photo of each child.

## Deciding to leave

Think about leaving when authorities or aid organizations advise it, when fighting comes closer, or when water, food, medicine or medical care has run out. Leaving is also dangerous: roads can be shelled or mined. Leave early, in daylight, on known routes, with water, medicines and documents. See [Family Emergency Plan](../reference/family-plan.md).

**Humanitarian corridors** are routes and times agreed between the warring sides so civilians can leave or aid can enter. Use only official information about them. They can still be dangerous, and agreements can break down.

Civilians who stay behind are still civilians. Under international humanitarian law, they must not be attacked.

For what becomes scarce and valuable in a long siege, see [What Is Worth the Most When Everything Stops](../reference/most-valuable.md).

## Protected emblems and humanitarian help

- The **Red Cross, Red Crescent and Red Crystal** emblems mark protected medical services and humanitarian workers. Under the Geneva Conventions they must not be attacked. Hospitals, ambulances and health workers are protected too.
- Do not use these emblems yourself unless you are authorized. Misuse puts everyone in danger and is forbidden.
- **Humanitarian aid is free.** Nobody may demand money, favors or sex for it. Report anyone who does.
- If you are forced from your home, register with the authorities or the UN refugee agency (UNHCR) to receive help.

## Finding missing family

The ICRC and National Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies run **Restoring Family Links**, a free service to trace missing and detained relatives and pass messages. Go to any Red Cross or Red Crescent office with names, dates of birth, the last known address and photos.

## Protecting children

- Keep children with you. Agree on a meeting place in case you are separated. Teach them their full name, their parents' names and a phone number.
- **Never hand children to strangers or groups who promise to take them to safety.**
- Armed groups and traffickers target children who are alone, poor or out of school. Recruiting children under 15 is a war crime. Keep children busy in school or activities, and watch for anyone offering them money or status.
- If you find a child alone, take them to the Red Cross or Red Crescent, UNICEF or the authorities so their family can be traced.
- Keep routines and play. See [Keeping Children Safe and Calm](../community/children-safety.md) and [Psychological First Aid](../community/psychological-first-aid.md).
