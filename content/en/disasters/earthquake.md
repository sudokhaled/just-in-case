> [!CAUTION]
> **Do not run outside while the ground is shaking.** Most earthquake injuries happen when people try to move during the shaking. They fall, or are hit by glass, bricks and falling walls near doors and outside walls. Drop where you are, take cover and hold on until the shaking stops.
>
> **At the coast, strong or long shaking means a tsunami may come within minutes.** As soon as the shaking stops, go to high ground on foot. Do not wait for an official warning.
>
> **If you smell gas after the quake, get out.** Do not use light switches, phones, matches or lighters inside. One spark can cause an explosion.

## Quick steps

1. **Drop** to your hands and knees before the shaking knocks you down.
2. **Cover** your head and neck with one arm. Crawl under a strong table or desk if one is a few steps away. If not, get low next to an inside wall, away from windows.
3. **Hold on** to the table (or to your head and neck) until the shaking stops. If the table moves, move with it.
4. **When the shaking stops,** check yourself and others for injuries. Put on strong shoes: the floor will be covered with broken glass.
5. **At the coast after strong or long shaking,** walk to high ground now. See [Tsunamis](../disasters/tsunami.md).
6. **Leave a damaged building** by the stairs, never the elevator (lift). Expect aftershocks.
7. **If you smell gas,** leave at once. Turn the gas off at the main valve only if you can do it safely.

## During the shaking

Shaking can last from a few seconds to several minutes. Stay where you are until it stops completely.

### Indoors

- Stay inside. Do not run to another room or outside.
- Get away from windows, glass doors, mirrors, tall furniture, shelves and anything hanging from the ceiling.
- If there is no table, get down next to an inside wall with nothing above you that can fall. Cover your head and neck with both arms.
- In a tall building, stay on your floor. Fire alarms and sprinklers may go off. Never use the elevator.

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| In bed | Stay in bed. Lie face down and cover your head and neck with a pillow. Hold on until the shaking stops. |
| In a wheelchair | Lock the wheels. Bend forward and cover your head and neck with your arms, a pillow or a book. Hold on. |
| Using a walker or cane | If you cannot get down, sit in a chair or on a bed. Bend forward and cover your head and neck. |
| In a shop, cinema or hall | Drop and cover where you are, between seats or away from shelves. Do not run for the exit with the crowd. |

### Outdoors

- If you can do it in a few steps, move away from buildings, walls, trees, streetlights and power lines. Then drop, cover your head and neck, and hold on.
- The most dangerous place outside is right next to a building. Glass, bricks and decorations fall from the walls.

### Driving

1. Slow down and stop in a clear place, away from bridges, overpasses, buildings, trees and power lines.
2. Set the parking brake. Stay inside with your seatbelt on until the shaking stops.
3. Drive on slowly. Watch for cracks, fallen rocks and damaged roads. Do not cross a bridge or overpass that may be damaged.
4. If a power line falls on the car, stay inside and wait for help.

### At the coast

If you are near the sea and the shaking is **strong enough that it is hard to stand**, or it **lasts 20 seconds or more**, a tsunami may follow. Protect yourself during the shaking. As soon as it stops, go on foot to high ground or far inland. Do not wait for sirens, officials or the sea to pull back. A tsunami from a nearby earthquake can arrive in 10 minutes or less.

## Myths that injure people

- **"Stand in a doorway."** Wrong. In most modern buildings a doorway is no stronger than any other part of the house. The door can swing and hit you. You are safer under a table.
- **"Triangle of life": lie down next to furniture, not under it.** Wrong. Earthquake safety agencies and rescue organizations reject this idea. Nobody can predict where empty spaces will form in a collapse, and moving across a shaking room causes injuries. Drop, Cover, Hold On is still the advice.
- **"Run outside."** Wrong. People are killed by falling glass and walls next to buildings, and by falls on stairs.
- **"Animals or the weather can predict earthquakes."** No method can predict earthquakes. Some places (for example Japan, Mexico and the US West Coast) have early warning systems that give a few seconds of warning after a quake starts. Warning systems and their alerts differ by country. If your phone, radio or a siren warns you, drop, cover and hold on at once.

## After the shaking

### Aftershocks

Expect aftershocks for days or weeks. Some are strong enough to bring down buildings that were damaged by the first quake. Each time, drop, cover and hold on. Rarely, the first earthquake is followed by a bigger one.

### Gas leaks and fire

1. If you smell gas (often like rotten eggs) or hear hissing, open the door and leave the building at once. Warn others.
2. Do not switch lights on or off. Do not use phones, matches, lighters or candles inside. Do not start a car in an attached garage.
3. If you can do it safely, turn off the gas at the main valve outside. Most valves close with a quarter turn, so the handle points across the pipe. Use a wrench. For a gas cylinder, close the valve on top.
4. **Only turn off the gas if you smell gas or suspect a leak.** Once it is off, a trained gas worker should turn it back on. Many homes lose gas for weeks after a quake because people closed valves that were fine.
5. If you see sparks or broken wires, turn off the electricity at the main switch, but only if you are not standing in water.
6. Small fires are common after earthquakes, and broken water pipes can leave firefighters without water. Put out small fires quickly. See [Wildfires and House Fires](../disasters/wildfire.md).

### Damaged buildings

- Get out if you see large cracks in walls or columns, leaning walls, a sagging roof or floor, or if the building has moved on its base.
- Do not go back inside until the building has been checked. A damaged building that looks fine can collapse in an aftershock.
- Outside, stay away from chimneys, brick walls, damaged buildings and fallen power lines.
- If you cannot go home, sleep outside or in a tent, away from buildings.

### If you are trapped

1. Stay as still as you can. Moving stirs up dust and can shift the rubble.
2. Cover your mouth and nose with cloth to filter the dust.
3. **Tap on a pipe or a wall** with a stone or piece of metal, in groups of 3, so rescuers can hear you. Use a whistle if you have one. See [Morse Code and Signal Codes](../comms/morse-code.md).
4. **Do not shout all the time.** It wastes your strength and makes you breathe in dust. Shout only when you hear rescuers close by.
5. Do not light matches or lighters. Gas may be leaking.
6. If you have a phone, send a text message with your location. Texts often get through when calls fail. Save the battery.
7. Rescue teams often stop all machines and call for silence so they can listen. When it goes quiet, tap.

### Searching for others

- Help the people near you first, in places you can reach safely.
- **Do not go into a building that is badly damaged or partly collapsed.** Untrained rescuers are often killed or trapped themselves. See [Finding and Freeing Trapped People](../navigation/search-rescue.md).
- Call out, then be completely quiet and listen for voices or tapping.
- Remove light rubble by hand, piece by piece, from the top of the pile. Do not pull pieces from the bottom: the pile can collapse onto the person.
- A person trapped for hours with a crushed arm, leg or body can collapse suddenly when the weight is lifted (crush syndrome). If any medical help exists, get it before or during the rescue.
- Treat bleeding first. See [Stopping Severe Bleeding](../medical/severe-bleeding.md).

## Before an earthquake

- **Fix tall furniture to the wall:** bookcases, wardrobes, cupboards and fridges. Use straps or metal L-brackets screwed into solid wall or studs.
- Strap the water heater to the wall so it cannot fall and break the gas or water pipe.
- Put heavy and breakable things on low shelves. Fit latches on cupboard doors.
- Do not hang heavy pictures or mirrors over beds or sofas. Move beds away from windows.
- Keep strong shoes and a flashlight next to every bed.
- Learn where and how to turn off the gas, water and electricity. Keep a wrench next to the gas valve.
- Practice Drop, Cover, Hold On with everyone in the family, including children, until they do it without thinking.
- Choose a meeting place outside and a contact person in another town. Pack a grab bag with water, food, medicines, a whistle and a first aid kit. See [Family Emergency Plan](../reference/family-plan.md).
- Find out what earthquake and tsunami warnings sound like where you live. Warning systems and terms differ by country.
