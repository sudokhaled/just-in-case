> [!CAUTION]
> **If you feel a strong or long earthquake at the coast, go to high ground now.** Do not wait for an official warning. A tsunami from a nearby earthquake can arrive in minutes, before any warning is sent.
>
> **If the sea suddenly pulls back, or you hear a loud roar from the ocean, run inland or uphill.** Never walk out to look at the exposed sea floor.
>
> **The first wave is often not the biggest.** Waves can keep coming for many hours. Do not go back down until officials say it is safe.

## Quick steps

1. **Natural warning = go now.** Strong shaking (hard to stand), shaking that lasts 20 seconds or more, the sea pulling back, or a roar from the ocean.
2. **Go on foot,** uphill or inland. Follow tsunami evacuation signs if there are any.
3. **Aim for at least 30 m (100 ft) above sea level, or 3 km (2 miles) inland,** if you can reach it. If not, go as high and as far as you can. Every meter helps.
4. **No high ground in reach?** Climb to the third floor or higher of a strong concrete building, or onto its roof.
5. **Stay there** until officials give the all-clear, even if the sea looks calm.
6. **Caught in the water?** Grab something that floats and climb onto it.

## Natural warning signs

| Sign | What it means |
|---|---|
| An earthquake near the coast that makes it hard to stand, or lasts 20 seconds or more | It may have moved the sea floor. A tsunami can arrive within minutes. |
| The sea pulls back fast, showing the sea floor, reefs or stranded boats | Water is being drawn into the first wave. It may arrive within minutes. |
| The sea rises fast, like a tide coming in at high speed, or a wall of white water | The wave is arriving. |
| A loud roar from the sea, like a train or a jet plane | The wave is close. |

**Any one of these signs means go.** Do not wait to see a second sign.

You may feel no earthquake at all. A tsunami from an earthquake on the other side of the ocean can arrive many hours later, with no shaking where you are. That is when official warnings save lives.

## Official warnings

- Tsunami warnings come from national agencies and from regional warning centers in the Pacific, Indian Ocean, Caribbean and Mediterranean. They reach people by sirens, radio, TV, phone alerts, loudspeakers or police.
- Warning levels and words (such as "warning", "advisory", "watch" or "threat") differ by country. Learn what yours mean before you need them.
- A warning for a distant earthquake may give you hours. Use the time to leave, not to pack.
- **No siren does not mean no danger.** Sirens fail, and there may be no time to send a warning after a nearby earthquake. Trust the natural signs.

## Where to go

- **High ground:** at least 30 m (100 ft) above sea level, or 3 km (2 miles) inland, if you can reach it. Local tsunami maps and signs take priority over these general numbers.
- **On foot.** Roads jam quickly. In Japan in 2011, many people died in cars stuck in traffic. Use a vehicle only if you must travel far, or for people who cannot walk, and leave at once.
- **Away from rivers.** Tsunamis travel up rivers and streams far inland. Do not stand on river banks or bridges near the sea.
- **Vertical evacuation (last resort):** if you cannot reach high ground in time, climb to the third floor or higher, or the roof, of a strong reinforced concrete building. Some coastal towns mark official tsunami evacuation buildings. Wooden houses are swept away.
- **Very last resort:** climb a tall, strong tree.
- **On a boat:** in deep water far from shore, a tsunami is small, so stay out at sea. In a harbor, it is usually safer to leave the boat and go to high ground. Follow harbor officials.

## Plan ahead

- Find out if your home, work and school are in a tsunami hazard zone. Many coastal areas have maps and route signs. See [Living by the Sea](../land/coast-living.md).
- Find the nearest high ground. Walk the route and time it. Practice it in the dark.
- Agree on a family meeting place on high ground. Teach everyone to go to high ground on their own. **Do not go back toward the coast to find family.** Schools in hazard zones should have their own plan to move children uphill.
- Keep a grab bag by the door. See [Emergency Kit Checklists](../reference/emergency-kits.md).

## If you are caught in the water

- Grab anything that floats: a log, a door, a tire, a cooler, furniture.
- Climb on top of it and hold on. Do not fight the current. Protect your head from debris.
- When the water slows, grab something fixed, such as a strong building or a tree, and climb up out of the water.
- The water flowing back out to sea is just as strong. Hold on.

## After the waves

- **Stay on high ground until officials announce the all-clear.** This can take many hours, sometimes more than a day. The sea can look calm between waves.
- If another strong earthquake happens, go to high ground again.
- Expect debris with nails, glass and sharp metal, collapsed buildings, gas leaks, fires and fallen power lines. Mud hides holes. Wear strong shoes and gloves.
- **Assume all water is contaminated.** Wells, tanks and pipes fill with seawater and sewage. Boiling and bleach kill germs, but they do not remove salt. See [Making Water Safe to Drink](../water/purify-water.md).
- **Clean every wound carefully.** Wounds from tsunami debris very often become infected. See [Cleaning and Caring for Wounds](../medical/wounds.md).
- People who swallowed or breathed in tsunami water can develop a serious lung infection days later. Cough, fever or trouble breathing after a near-drowning need medical care.
- Search for others only in places that are safe to reach. See [Earthquakes](../disasters/earthquake.md) for damaged buildings and gas leaks.
- Shock and grief are normal. See [Psychological First Aid](../community/psychological-first-aid.md).
