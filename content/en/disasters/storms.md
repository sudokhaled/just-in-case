> [!CAUTION]
> **Water kills more people than wind.** Storm surge (the sea pushed onto land by the storm) and flooding from rain cause most hurricane and cyclone deaths. If you are told to leave a surge or flood zone, leave. Run from the water, hide from the wind.
>
> **The calm in the eye of the storm is not the end.** The wind comes back, often within an hour, from the other direction. Stay inside until officials say the storm has passed.
>
> **Never run a generator, grill or stove indoors after the storm.** Carbon monoxide kills whole families in their sleep.

## Quick steps

1. **Listen for official warnings.** Leave early if you are in an evacuation zone, near the coast or a river, or in a mobile home, caravan, tent or weak building.
2. **If you stay,** cover windows, bring in loose objects, and store water, food and medicines for several days.
3. **During a hurricane or cyclone,** stay in an inside room on the lowest floor that will not flood, away from windows.
4. **Tornado warning or a loud roar:** go to a basement or storm shelter, or a small inside room on the lowest floor. Cover your head and neck. Get under a mattress or a heavy table.
5. **Thunder:** if you hear it, go inside a solid building or a hard-topped car. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder.
6. **After the storm:** stay away from fallen power lines and flood water. Run generators outdoors only.

## Names and warnings

Hurricanes, typhoons and cyclones are the same kind of storm. The name depends on the region.

| Region | Name used |
|---|---|
| North Atlantic, Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico, eastern and central North Pacific | Hurricane |
| Northwest Pacific (East and Southeast Asia) | Typhoon |
| Indian Ocean, South Pacific, Australia | Cyclone or tropical cyclone |

Warning systems, strength scales and words differ by country. In the US, a **watch** means dangerous conditions are possible, and a **warning** means they are expected. Learn what your local sirens, flags or alerts mean. A tornado warning may give only a few minutes.

## Before a hurricane or cyclone

### Prepare your home

- Cover windows with storm shutters, or with plywood at least 16 mm (5/8 in) thick, cut to size and screwed into the frame.
- **Tape on windows is useless.** It does not stop glass breaking and can make larger, more dangerous pieces.
- Bring inside or tie down anything the wind can throw: furniture, bins, bicycles, roofing sheets and gas cylinders. Cut dead branches near the house.
- Fill containers with drinking water, and a bath or buckets for washing and flushing.
- Charge phones and power banks. Fill the car with fuel. Get some cash. Turn the fridge and freezer to their coldest setting.
- Put documents and medicines in waterproof bags. Pack a grab bag. See [Emergency Kit Checklists](../reference/emergency-kits.md).

### Leave or stay?

| Leave if | Staying may be possible if |
|---|---|
| Officials tell you to leave | You are outside surge and flood zones |
| You live in a storm surge zone or near a river | Your building is strong and well built |
| You live in a mobile home, caravan, tent or shack | You have supplies for several days |
| You need electricity for medical equipment | No evacuation order has been given |

Leave early, in daylight, before roads flood. Often you only need to reach higher ground or go tens of kilometers inland. Public cyclone shelters, as in Bangladesh and India, have saved many thousands of lives.

## During a hurricane or cyclone

- Stay inside, away from windows and glass doors. Use an inside room, closet or hallway on the lowest floor that will not flood.
- If water starts coming in, go to a higher floor. Do not go into a closed attic, where rising water can trap you. See [Floods](../disasters/flood.md).
- If the building starts to break apart, lie on the floor under a heavy table and cover your head.
- **Do not go outside when the eye passes over.** The wind returns suddenly from the opposite direction.
- Listen to a battery or crank radio for updates. See [Emergency Radio](../comms/emergency-radio.md).

Storm surge can raise the sea by several meters within minutes. In the United States from 1963 to 2012, about half of hurricane deaths were from storm surge and a quarter from rain flooding. Since then, rain flooding, often far inland, has killed the most. See [Living by the Sea](../land/coast-living.md).

## Tornadoes

**Warning signs:** a dark, often greenish sky, large hail, a loud continuous roar like a train, or a rotating funnel or cloud of debris near the ground. Rain or darkness can hide a tornado. Do not wait to see one.

### Where to shelter

1. **Best:** a storm cellar, basement or purpose-built safe room.
2. **No basement:** a small room in the middle of the lowest floor, with no windows: a closet, bathroom or inside hallway. Put as many walls as possible between you and the outside.
3. Get under something strong, such as a heavy table or workbench. Cover yourself with a mattress, sleeping bags or thick blankets.
4. **Protect your head and neck** with your arms. Wear a helmet (bicycle, motorcycle or sports) if you have one: head injuries cause many tornado deaths. Put on strong shoes.
5. In a tall building, go to an inside hallway on the lowest floor you can reach. Do not use the elevator.

### Places that are not safe

- **Mobile homes and caravans,** even when tied down. Leave for a strong building before the tornado arrives.
- **Vehicles.** There is no safe option. As a last resort, stay buckled in with your head below the windows, covered with your arms or a blanket, or lie flat in a ditch clearly lower than the road and cover your head.
- **Highway overpasses and bridges. Never shelter under them.** The wind speeds up underneath and blows debris through.
- Halls with wide roofs, such as gyms and supermarkets. Go to a small inside room such as a toilet.
- **Do not open windows** to "equalize pressure". It is a myth and lets in flying glass.

## Lightning

- **When thunder roars, go indoors.** If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.
- **30-30 rule:** count the seconds from the flash to the thunder. If it is less than 30 seconds, the storm is within about 10 km (6 miles): go inside now. After the storm, wait **30 minutes** after the last thunder before going out.
- **Safe:** a large building with wiring and plumbing, or a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows closed. **Not safe:** tents, open shelters, small sheds, under trees.
- Inside, do not use corded phones, shower, or touch electrical equipment during the storm.

### If there is no shelter (last resort)

There is no safe place outside in a thunderstorm. These steps reduce the risk a little:

- Get off hills, ridges and peaks. Get out of the water and off beaches.
- Stay away from tall single trees, poles, metal fences and power lines.
- In a group, spread out at least 6 m (20 ft) apart so one strike does not hurt everyone. Do not lie flat on the ground.
- The "lightning crouch" (squat on the balls of your feet, feet together, head down, hands over ears) may reduce the risk slightly, but it does not make you safe. The US National Weather Service no longer recommends it. It is better to keep moving toward a building or car.

**People struck by lightning do not carry an electric charge.** It is safe to touch them. If they are not breathing, start CPR at once. Many survive with fast CPR. See [CPR: When Someone Is Not Breathing](../medical/cpr.md).

## After the storm

- Stay inside until officials say the danger has passed. Many people die after the storm, not during it.
- **Fallen power lines:** treat every wire as live. Stay at least 10 m (33 ft) away from it and from anything it touches, such as fences, puddles and cars. If a line falls on your car, stay inside. If fire forces you out, jump clear with your feet together, never touching the car and the ground at the same time, then shuffle away with your feet together.
- **Flood water:** do not walk or drive through it.
- **Chainsaws** cause many serious injuries after storms. Wear a helmet, eye and ear protection, gloves and strong boots. Never cut above shoulder height, from a ladder, alone or when tired. Watch for kickback when the tip of the bar touches wood. Branches under tension can spring back with great force.
- **Generators:** run them outdoors only, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents, never in a house, garage or basement. The same applies to grills and camp stoves. Headache, dizziness and nausea in several people at once mean carbon monoxide: get everyone into fresh air. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).
- Watch for gas leaks, damaged buildings, nails and broken glass. Treat drinking water if supplies were damaged.
