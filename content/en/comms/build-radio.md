> [!CAUTION]
> **Keep every antenna far from power lines.** Put it where no part can touch a line if it falls. Never throw a wire or rope over or near a line. Touching a power line kills.
>
> **Disconnect outdoor antennas when you hear thunder,** and never touch them during a storm. Lightning can run down the wire into the house.
>
> **Never run a car engine in a garage or closed space to power the radio.** The exhaust gas (carbon monoxide) kills in minutes. Use low-voltage batteries only: never connect anything you build to mains power.

## Quick steps

1. **Try the radios you have first:** a battery or crank radio, the car radio (key in the accessory position, engine off, 10 to 15 minutes at a time), or a phone FM radio app with **wired** headphones plugged in as the antenna.
2. **Listen to AM (medium wave) at night.** After dark, AM stations can be heard hundreds of kilometers away.
3. **Add a long wire to hear farther.** Run 10 to 30 m (33 to 100 ft) of any wire outdoors, as high as you can, and clip one end to the radio's antenna.
4. **To build a radio that needs no battery (a crystal radio),** collect: a long antenna wire, a ground wire to a metal rod in damp soil, a coil of 80 to 100 turns of thin wire on a 5 cm (2 in) tube, a germanium diode (1N34A or similar) and a high-impedance crystal or piezo earpiece.
5. **Connect it:** antenna to the top of the coil, ground to the bottom. Diode from the top of the coil to one earpiece wire. Other earpiece wire to ground. Tune by moving the antenna along a scraped strip of the coil, or with a variable capacitor across the coil.
6. **Do not build a transmitter.** To call for help, use a ready-made licence-free or emergency radio. In a real life-or-death emergency, you may use any radio on any channel.

## Radios you already own

### Car radio

- Turn the key to the accessory position, or press the start button once without pressing the brake or clutch. Keep the engine off.
- Listen for 10 to 15 minutes at a time. Many cars also switch on fans, computers and lights. A flat battery will not start the engine.
- To recharge the battery, run the engine only outdoors, away from doors, windows and vents. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).

### Phones with an FM radio

- Some Android phones have an FM receiver chip. Look for an app called "FM Radio" or "Radio". iPhones do not have one.
- It needs **wired** headphones plugged in: the cable is the antenna. Bluetooth headphones do not work.
- It needs no mobile network. On many phones it keeps working in airplane mode, which saves battery.

### Scanners and other receivers

- **Scanners** receive walkie-talkies, marine and aviation radio, amateur radio repeaters and weather radio. Many police, fire and ambulance services now use digital or encrypted radio, which older scanners cannot hear.
- **A shortwave radio with an SSB switch,** or a software-defined radio (SDR) USB stick with a laptop, also hears Morse code and amateur voice. Install SDR software before you need it.
- In some countries it is illegal to listen to some services or to repeat what you hear. Do not spread private messages.

## Making any radio hear farther

### A long-wire antenna

1. **Use 10 to 30 m (33 to 100 ft) of any wire,** insulated or bare. Never use an electric fence.
2. **Run it high and straight,** at least 3 m (10 ft) above the ground, for example from a window to a tree. Height helps more than length.
3. **Tie the ends with plastic rope or nylon cord,** not wire, so the antenna does not touch trees or walls.
4. **Bring the end to the radio:**
   - Antenna socket or screw: connect it there.
   - Telescopic antenna: clip or wrap the bare end around it. On FM, a long wire can overload the radio: use 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft).
   - Pocket AM radio with no telescopic antenna: wind 5 to 10 turns of insulated wire around the case. Connect one end to the antenna and the other to a ground wire.
5. **Keep it away from noise:** LED bulbs, chargers, solar inverters and computers make hiss and buzz.

### A good ground

- Drive a metal rod or pipe at least 1 m (3 ft) into damp soil. Pour water around it in dry weather.
- Or clamp the wire to a metal cold-water pipe that runs into the ground. Many pipes are plastic: check.
- **Never use gas pipes. Never use the earth pin of a wall socket or any part of the house wiring.**

### A dipole for one band

A dipole is 2 equal wires in a straight line, fed at the middle. It hears best from its sides.

- **Total length in meters = 143 / frequency in MHz.** Each half is half of that. (In feet: 468 / frequency in MHz.)
- **Shortwave example:** for a station on 6.1 MHz, 143 / 6.1 = **23.4 m (77 ft)** in total, so 2 wires of **11.7 m (38 ft)** each.
- **FM example:** for 100 MHz, 143 / 100 = **1.43 m (4 ft 8 in)** in total, 72 cm (28 in) each side. It works across the whole FM band.
- For AM medium wave, a dipole would be 84 to 270 m (275 to 885 ft) long. Use a long wire instead.

1. Tie the inner ends of the 2 wires to a small piece of plastic, 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) apart.
2. Connect a 2-wire cable (TV coax or speaker wire): one conductor to each half. At the radio, connect one conductor to the antenna and the other to the ground terminal, if there is one.
3. Hang it high and level, side-on to the station if you know its direction.

## Building a crystal radio (no battery)

A crystal radio is powered only by the radio waves it catches. The coil and capacitor pick out one station. The diode turns the radio signal into a sound signal. A sensitive earpiece makes it faintly audible.

**What it can hear:** strong AM medium-wave stations, usually within about 40 km (25 miles). With a long, high antenna and a good ground, it may pick up very powerful stations 100 km (60 miles) or more away, mostly at night, when medium-wave signals travel farther. It cannot receive FM. Where AM stations have closed, as in much of Europe, there may be nothing to hear.

### Parts

| Part | Best | Improvised or scavenged |
|---|---|---|
| Antenna | 20 to 30 m (65 to 100 ft) of wire, high | Any wire or cable |
| Ground | Metal rod 1 m (3 ft) into damp soil | Metal water pipe, buried metal sheet |
| Coil | 80 to 100 turns of enamelled copper wire, 0.3 to 0.5 mm (24 to 28 AWG), on a 5 cm (2 in) tube | Thin insulated wire; wire from a dead relay or motor; the coil on the ferrite rod of an old AM radio |
| Tuning | Variable capacitor of about 365 pF from an old AM radio | A sliding contact, or taps every 10 turns |
| Diode | Germanium: 1N34A, 1N60, OA90, OA91, AA119 | Schottky (1N5711, BAT85); silicon 1N4148 hears only very strong stations |
| Earpiece | Crystal (piezo) earpiece | Piezo disc from a buzzer or musical greeting card; headphones marked 2000 ohms or more |
| Resistor | 47 to 100 kilohms across the earpiece | Any value from 10 to 100 kilohms |

- **Ordinary headphones (16 to 32 ohms) do not work.** They need more power than a crystal radio gives.
- **Old radios and TVs** contain diodes, tuning capacitors, coils and wire.
- **No diode at all:** a rusty or blued razor blade, touched lightly with a pencil lead or safety pin point, can work as a detector (the wartime "foxhole radio"). It is weak: move the point until you find a sensitive spot.

### Winding the coil

1. Use a cardboard tube about 5 cm (2 in) across, such as a kitchen-roll tube. On a narrower toilet-roll tube, wind about 100 turns.
2. Anchor the wire through 2 small holes near one end. Leave 30 cm (1 ft) free.
3. Wind 80 to 100 turns tightly, side by side, all in the same direction. Do not let turns cross. For tuning by taps, twist a small loop every 10 turns.
4. Anchor the far end through 2 more holes. Leave 30 cm (1 ft) free.
5. Scrape the enamel off both ends with a knife or sandpaper until the copper shines.
6. For a sliding contact, sand a strip about 5 mm (1/4 in) wide along the coil, just through the enamel on top of each turn.

### Connecting it

```
  ANTENNA
     |
     A------------+-------------+
     |            |             |
     3            |            _|_
     3  COIL     ===  TUNING   \ /  DIODE
     3           ===  CAP      -+-
     3            |             |
     |            |             B------+
     |            |             |      |
     |            |            [R]    [E]
     |            |             |      |
     G------------+-------------+------+
     |
  GROUND

  R = resistor   E = earpiece
```

1. Connect the antenna to A, the top of the coil, and the ground to G, the bottom.
2. Connect the tuning capacitor between A and G, if you have one.
3. Connect the diode from A to B. Either way round works.
4. Connect the earpiece between B and G, and the resistor across the earpiece.
5. Fix the parts to a wooden board. Twist every joint tight and scrape every contact clean.
6. **No capacitor:** connect the antenna to a sliding contact (a safety pin or springy brass strip pressed on the sanded strip) instead of A.

### Using it

1. Put the earpiece in your ear, in a quiet place. Tune slowly.
2. **Nothing heard:** check the ground and every joint, try a longer and higher antenna, try another diode, and try again after dark.
3. **2 stations mixed:** connect the antenna to a tap lower down the coil, or use a shorter antenna.

## Why ordinary radios cannot hear Morse or most amateur radio

- **Morse code (CW) by radio** is a plain signal switched on and off. An AM radio turns it into silence or soft clicks. You need a receiver marked "BFO", "SSB", "USB/LSB" or "CW", which turns it into a tone. To learn to read it, see [Sending and Receiving Morse With What You Have](../comms/morse-devices.md).
- **Much amateur voice on shortwave uses SSB** (single sideband). On an AM radio it sounds like fast, garbled, duck-like speech. Switch to SSB and tune very slowly until the voice sounds normal.

## Power for radios

- **Use an earphone** and low volume. A speaker uses far more power.
- Most small radios run for tens of hours on a set of alkaline AA cells. NiMH rechargeable cells and USB power banks also work.
- **Use the right voltage.** 4 AA cells make 6 V. Never connect a 6 V radio to a 12 V battery.
- Listen at fixed times and switch off between them. See [Batteries: Use, Charge and Stay Safe](../power/batteries.md) and [Electricity Without the Grid](../power/off-grid-power.md).

## Lightning and power lines

- **When thunder is near, or when you go away,** disconnect the antenna. Connect it to the ground rod outside, or put its end outside, away from the house.
- Fallen power lines can be live, or come back on without warning. Stay at least 10 m (33 ft) away.

## Why you should not build a transmitter

- **It is illegal without a licence in almost every country.**
- **Home-made transmitters jam other people.** Spark-gap sets, simple one-transistor transmitters and home-made amplifiers spread noise over many frequencies, including aviation, marine and emergency channels.
- **You do not need one.** Licence-free radios (PMR446, FRS, UHF CB and CB) are cheap and legal. To text without a network, see [Text Messages Without Internet: Mesh Radios and Phones](../comms/mesh-networks.md).
- **To build transmitters, get an amateur radio licence.** Licensed amateurs may build their own equipment.
- **In a real life-or-death emergency,** you may use any ready-made radio on any channel to call for help. For the channels and the exact words, see [Emergency Radio](../comms/emergency-radio.md).
