> [!CAUTION]
> **In a real life-or-death emergency, you may use any radio on any channel to call for help.** Say MAYDAY 3 times, then say where you are and what help you need.
>
> **Do not trust the range printed on the box.** A walkie-talkie sold as "50 km" often reaches only 1 to 3 km among buildings or trees. Get high to be heard.
>
> **Never make a false distress call.** It is a crime in most countries and can take rescuers away from someone who is dying.

## Quick steps

### To get news

1. Try **AM (medium wave)** first. At night, AM stations can be heard hundreds of kilometers away.
2. Try **FM** for local news and **shortwave** for international news.
3. Listen at fixed times, such as the start of each hour, and turn the radio off between them to save power.

### To call for help

1. Use the emergency channel of the radio you have: **marine VHF channel 16, CB channel 9, UHF CB channel 5** (Australia), or any channel where you hear people talking.
2. Get as high as you can. Hold the radio upright.
3. Listen first. Then press the button, wait 1 second, and speak slowly.
4. Say: MAYDAY 3 times, who you are, where you are, what happened, how many people, what you need, then OVER.
5. Release the button and listen for at least 1 minute. If nobody answers, repeat every few minutes.

## Receiving: which band to try

| Band | Typical range | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| AM, medium wave (about 530-1700 kHz) | Up to a few hundred km by day; much farther at night | National and regional news, official announcements |
| FM (87.5-108 MHz; 76-95 MHz in Japan) | About 50-100 km, line of sight | Local news, clear sound |
| Shortwave (3-30 MHz) | Thousands of km | International broadcasters, news from other countries |
| Weather radio (US and Canada, 162.400-162.550 MHz) | About 60 km (40 miles) | Official weather and hazard warnings |

- **Shortwave:** below about 10 MHz works best at night, above 10 MHz by day. Search the broadcast bands: 5.9-6.2 MHz and 7.2-7.45 MHz at night; 9.4-9.9 MHz, 11.6-12.1 MHz and 15.1-15.8 MHz by day.
- **Weather radio:** NOAA Weather Radio (US) broadcasts on 7 channels from 162.400 to 162.550 MHz. Other countries use broadcast radio, sirens or phone alerts. Warning systems differ by country.
- **Crank and solar radios:** 1 minute of cranking often gives only a few minutes of listening, and small solar panels charge slowly. A radio that also takes AA batteries is more reliable.
- **Better reception:** pull the antenna out fully. For AM, turn the whole radio: the antenna inside picks up best in one direction. For shortwave, clip a long wire (5 to 20 m, or 15 to 65 ft) to the antenna and run it high outdoors. Move away from electronics that cause noise, such as LED bulbs and chargers.

## Transmitting: which radio, which channel

| Radio | Where used | Licence | Emergency or calling channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| FRS walkie-talkie (462-467 MHz) | USA, Canada and parts of the Americas | None | No official channel: scan for voices |
| GMRS (462-467 MHz) | USA | Licence needed (no exam in the US) | No official channel: scan for voices |
| PMR446 (446.0-446.2 MHz) | Europe and many other countries | None | No official channel: scan for voices |
| UHF CB (476-477 MHz) | Australia, New Zealand | None | Channel 5 (repeater pair 35) in Australia |
| CB radio (27 MHz) | Worldwide, rules vary | None in most countries | Channel 9 emergency; channel 19 used by drivers in the US |
| Marine VHF | Sea, coasts, big lakes and rivers | Usually required | Channel 16 (156.800 MHz) |
| Aviation airband | Aircraft and airports | Required | 121.5 MHz |
| Amateur (ham) radio | Worldwide | Exam and licence required | 146.520 MHz (North America), 145.500 MHz (Europe) |

- **Licence-free radios** (FRS, PMR446, UHF CB) are cheap and simple, but range is short: often 1 to 5 km on flat ground, more from a hilltop.
- **Marine channel 16** is watched by coast guards and ships. It is the best choice near the sea or a large lake.
- **121.5 MHz** is the international aviation emergency frequency, monitored by many aircraft and air traffic units. You need an airband transmitter.
- **Rescue beacons** (PLB, EPIRB, ELT) send your position to satellites on 406 MHz. In remote places they are the most reliable way to call for help. See [Signaling for Rescue](../navigation/signal-rescue.md).

To send text messages without a phone network, see [Text Messages Without Internet: Mesh Radios and Phones](../comms/mesh-networks.md).

## Amateur (ham) radio basics

- Licensed amateur operators exist in almost every country. Many train to pass messages when phones fail.
- **2 m band** (around 144-148 MHz): local, line-of-sight contacts. Repeaters on hills can extend range to 50 km or more, but you need the right settings (frequency offset and often a tone).
- **Calling frequencies (simplex, no repeater):** 146.520 MHz in North America, 145.500 MHz in Europe.
- **HF bands** (for example 3.5-4.0 MHz and 7.0-7.3 MHz) reach across countries, especially at night, but need a large wire antenna.
- Many cheap handheld radios can transmit on amateur frequencies. Outside a real emergency, you need a licence. Get licensed and learn the local repeaters before a disaster.

## The law in a real emergency

- The **ITU Radio Regulations**, an international treaty, say that nothing in them stops a station in distress from using any means it has to attract attention, give its position and get help.
- In the US, the amateur radio rules (47 CFR 97.403 and 97.405) allow any means of radio communication when life is in immediate danger and normal communication is not available. Many countries have similar rules.
- A real emergency means **immediate danger to life or serious harm,** not convenience. When it ends, normal rules apply again. Never interfere with another station's emergency traffic.

## How to make a distress call

1. Choose the channel: marine 16, aviation 121.5 MHz, CB 9, UHF CB 5, a ham calling frequency or local repeater, or any channel with voices on it.
2. Listen for a few seconds. Do not talk over someone else's emergency.
3. Press and hold the talk button. Wait 1 second. Speak slowly and clearly, with the radio 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) from your mouth.
4. Say:
   - "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY."
   - "This is" and your name, call sign or boat name, 3 times.
   - **Where:** your position, using landmarks, a road, a GPS reading or a direction and distance from a known place.
   - **What:** what happened.
   - **How many:** the number of people, and any injuries.
   - **Needs:** the help you need.
   - "OVER."
5. Release the button and listen for at least 1 minute. If nobody answers, repeat every few minutes. If still nothing, try another channel or move higher.
6. If someone answers, answer their questions, repeat your position and stay on that channel. Save battery between calls.

Use **PAN-PAN** (said 3 times) instead of MAYDAY for an urgent problem that is not yet a threat to life. Marine radios with a red DISTRESS button send an automatic alarm, with your position if connected to GPS: lift the cover and hold the button until the radio beeps.

## Antennas: height matters

VHF and UHF signals (walkie-talkies, marine radio, 2 m ham radio, UHF CB) travel in straight lines. Hills, buildings and trees block them. **Getting higher is the best way to get more range.**

| Antenna height | Distance to the radio horizon, flat open ground |
|---|---|
| 1.5 m (5 ft) | About 5 km (3 miles) |
| 10 m (33 ft) | About 13 km (8 miles) |
| 100 m (330 ft) | About 41 km (25 miles) |

The ranges of the two radios add together, but real range is usually less because of obstacles and low power.

- Hold the radio upright, antenna vertical, away from your body and from metal.
- Go to a hilltop, roof or upper floor. If the signal is weak, move a few meters: small moves can make a big difference.
- Never transmit without the antenna attached. It can damage the radio.

To build a radio that needs no battery, or a better antenna for any radio, see [Building and Improvising Radios](../comms/build-radio.md).

## Batteries and listening schedules

- Transmitting uses many times more power than listening. Keep calls short. Use the low power setting when the other radio is close.
- Carry spare batteries. In the cold, keep them warm inside your clothes. Remove batteries from radios in storage so they do not leak. See [Batteries: Use, Charge and Stay Safe](../power/batteries.md).
- **Agree on a listening schedule** with family or your group before you need it, for example: "Channel 3, for 5 minutes at the start of every hour from 06:00 to 22:00." Write it on paper with the channels and keep it with the radio. See [Family Emergency Plan](../reference/family-plan.md).
- If you cannot speak, see [Morse Code and Signal Codes](../comms/morse-code.md).
