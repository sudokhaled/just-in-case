> [!CAUTION]
> **Untrained rescuers die in collapsed buildings.** Never enter a building that is leaning, partly collapsed or off its foundation. Never crawl into a rubble pile or under a heavy slab. That work is for trained teams with equipment.
>
> **A person trapped by an arm or leg for hours can die suddenly when the weight comes off** (crush syndrome). Get medical help before you free them, if you possibly can.
>
> **Never work alone.** Work in pairs, and always leave someone outside who knows where you are.

## Quick steps

1. **Check for danger:** gas smell, sparking wires, fire, flooding, leaning walls, large cracks. If the building is leaning or partly collapsed, stay out.
2. **Gather facts:** ask neighbors who was inside and where: bedrooms at night, kitchens at mealtimes.
3. **Call out:** "If anyone can hear me, shout or tap!" Then everyone goes silent and listens.
4. **Search in a pattern,** in pairs, room by room. Tell someone outside where you are.
5. **Mark the door** when you go in and when you come out.
6. **Free lightly trapped people first,** removing debris by hand from the top.
7. **Lift heavy objects only a few centimeters at a time,** pushing blocks underneath as you lift.
8. **Give first aid,** then move people to a safe place away from buildings.

## Neighbors save most lives

Most people rescued alive after an earthquake are freed by neighbors in the first hours. Some are found alive after more than a week. But untrained rescuers also die: after the 1985 Mexico City earthquake, volunteers rescued about 800 people, and about 100 volunteers were killed. See [Organizing a Community](../community/community-groups.md).

## Protect yourself first

- **Wear** a helmet (even a bicycle helmet), heavy gloves, strong boots, goggles and a dust mask. Carry a flashlight and a whistle.
- **Work in pairs.** One searches while the other watches the walls and ceiling.
- **Keep a leader outside** who writes down who went in, where and when. If a pair is late, nobody rushes in after them: the leader decides.
- **Whistle signals** used by rescue teams: **3 short blasts** means everyone out now. **1 long blast** means stop and be silent. **1 long and 1 short** means start work again.
- **Expect aftershocks.** Know your way out. Check the building again after each one. Use no flames: gas may be leaking.

## Size up the building

Walk all the way around it first. If you smell gas or hear hissing, turn it off at the main valve only if it is safe (see [Earthquakes](../disasters/earthquake.md)). Turn off electricity at the main switch, never while standing in water.

| Damage | What you see | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Broken windows, cracked plaster, furniture thrown around | Search inside with care. |
| Moderate | Many cracks in walls, fallen chimneys, but upright and on its foundation | A few people go in quickly, take out people easy to reach, and leave. |
| Heavy | Leaning, partly or fully collapsed, off its foundation, floors sagging | **Do not enter.** Call and listen from outside. Wait for trained teams. |

**Get out at once** if walls start to lean, new cracks appear, dust or plaster falls, doors jam suddenly, or you hear creaking. Large diagonal or X-shaped cracks in walls, and cracks in concrete columns or beams, mean the structure is badly weakened.

## Searching

1. **Call out:** "If anyone can hear my voice, come to me."
2. **Call, then listen.** Everyone stops work and stays silent for at least 30 seconds. Listen for voices, tapping or moaning. Tap 3 times on a pipe or wall and listen again.
3. **Find the sound.** Put 3 or 4 searchers at different places around the area. Each points toward the sound. Search where the lines cross.
4. **Search each room the same way.** Keep your right hand on the wall and go all the way around, checking under beds and tables, in cupboards and bathrooms, and under stairs. Then check the middle. If lost, follow the wall back with your left hand.
5. **Search floor by floor,** and never skip a room.

## Marking a searched building

On arrival, draw one diagonal slash next to the main door. When you leave, add the second slash to make an X, and fill in the 4 spaces. Use paint, chalk or charcoal, large enough to read from the street.

```
              1 NOV 15:40
                \     /
                 \   /
      TEAM 3      \ /      GAS LEAK
                   X       2ND FLOOR
                  / \      NOT SEARCHED
                 /   \
              2 L   1 D

  Top: date and time you left. Left: team.
  Right: hazards, places not searched.
  Bottom: people still inside
  (L = alive, D = dead, 0 = none).
```

Professional teams use their own marks, often a box shape. **Never change or paint over others' marks.** Mark the spot of a person you cannot free with a large arrow, and tell the next team.

## Freeing people from light debris

1. **Talk to the person.** Ask where they hurt and what is on top of them.
2. **Clear from the top, by hand, piece by piece.** Pass pieces back along a line of people to a pile that does not block exits.
3. **Never pull pieces from the bottom or sides** of a pile. It can collapse onto them or you.
4. **Uncover the head and chest first** so they can breathe. Clear dust from the mouth and nose.
5. **Give first aid** as soon as you can reach them. See [First Aid Basics: Check, Call, Care](../medical/first-aid-basics.md).

## Lifting with a lever and blocks

1. **Gather blocks:** short pieces of strong, square wood, for example 10 x 10 cm (4 x 4 in) and about 45 cm (18 in) long. Bricks and hollow concrete blocks can crack suddenly.
2. **Find a lever:** a steel bar or strong beam. Put a solid block under it as the pivot, close to the load. A car jack also works.
3. **Lift a little, block a little.** One person lifts slowly, about 2-3 cm (1 in) at a time. Another pushes blocks in right under the load. Lift only as high as you need to free the person.
4. **Stack the blocks in a box pattern,** like a log cabin: 2 blocks side by side, the next 2 across them at right angles.
5. **Lower the load onto the blocks** before anyone reaches in. **Never put any part of your body under a load held only by a lever or jack.**
6. **Slide the person out** along the length of their body. See [Moving and Carrying the Injured](../medical/moving-injured.md).

## Crush injury and crush syndrome

When a heavy weight presses on a large muscle (thigh, hip, calf, upper arm) for a long time, the muscle starts to die. When the weight comes off, poisons from it flood into the blood. This can stop the heart within minutes and damage the kidneys over days. The risk starts after about 1 hour and grows with time. The person may seem well while trapped, then collapse after release.

1. **Get medical help to the scene before release** if at all possible. Trained staff can give fluid into a vein before the weight comes off.
2. **While they are trapped,** if they are awake, can swallow and are not vomiting, give plenty of clean water, a little at a time. Avoid fruit juice and coconut water, which are rich in potassium.
3. **If medical help cannot come, free them anyway.** Leaving them trapped is also deadly. Be ready to start CPR the moment the weight comes off.
4. Use a tourniquet only to stop severe bleeding. See [Stopping Severe Bleeding](../medical/severe-bleeding.md).
5. **After release,** lay them flat, keep them warm, and watch breathing and pulse closely for the first hour. Keep the freed limb at heart level. Do not raise or rub it.
6. **Keep giving water and watch the urine.** Dark brown or red urine, or very little urine, means the kidneys are failing. Evacuate to a hospital that can do dialysis, even days later.

Without a hospital, some people with crush syndrome die even with the best first aid. Freeing them is still right. A crushed chest is different: free it at once. See [Chest and Belly Injuries](../medical/chest-injuries.md).

## Dust and asbestos

Collapse dust contains fine concrete and glass, and in older buildings often asbestos. These cause lung disease and cancer years later.

- Wear an N95, FFP2 or FFP3 mask. A cloth mask stops only coarse dust.
- Spray dust lightly with water before moving debris. Never sweep it dry.
- Give the trapped person a mask or a damp cloth.

## Many trapped, few rescuers

Save the most people who can be saved, in the shortest time.

1. **First:** people on the surface or lightly trapped.
2. **Next:** people you can reach by clearing light debris.
3. **Last:** people deep under heavy rubble. Mark the place, pass them water if you can, keep talking, and guide trained teams there.

Do not spend hours on one deep rescue while others nearby could be freed in minutes. Leave the dead in place for now and record where they are.
