> [!CAUTION]
> **Never drink seawater,** not even a little, not even mixed with fresh water. It makes dehydration worse.
>
> **A strong earthquake, or the sea suddenly pulling back, means run to high ground now.** A tsunami can arrive in minutes. See [Tsunamis](../disasters/tsunami.md).
>
> **Never eat mussels, clams, oysters or scallops from water you do not know,** especially in warm months, after a red tide, or when dead fish or birds lie on the beach. Cooking does not destroy the poison. It can stop your breathing within hours.

## Quick steps

1. **Sleep above the storm line,** out of the wind, away from cliffs, with a route to high ground you can walk in minutes.
2. **Learn the tides.** Most coasts have 2 high and 2 low tides a day. Each tide comes about 50 minutes later than the day before.
3. **Never let the tide cut you off** on a sandbar, rock or island, or below a cliff. Head back while the water is still going out.
4. **Get fresh water** from rain, streams above the reach of the tide, and shallow holes behind the dunes. Treat it.
5. **Gather food at low tide:** fish, crabs and seaweed first. Shellfish only from clean water with no red tide.
6. **Make salt** by letting seawater dry in shallow pans in the sun.
7. **Swim only with others.** In a rip current, swim parallel to the beach.

### Fish and shellfish

- Fish from rocks around high tide. Build a stone tidal trap on a gently sloping beach. Search rock pools at low tide. See [Catching Fish](../food/fishing.md).
- **Prefer fish, crabs and seaweed to mussels, clams and oysters.** Shellfish filter poison from the water and store it. There is no home test, and the water often looks normal.
- Never take shellfish near sewage outlets, harbors, river mouths below towns, or after floods. They carry cholera and hepatitis A.
- Tingling or numb lips after eating shellfish is poisoning. Stop eating at once. Be ready to give rescue breaths.

### Seaweed

People eat seaweed on coasts all over the world. Common kinds are laver or nori (thin purple-brown sheets on rocks), dulse (flat red fronds), sea lettuce (thin bright green sheets), kelps (large brown ribbons) and Irish moss (small, branched, dark red).

- **Pick living seaweed** still attached to rocks, in clean, moving water. Never eat seaweed washed up on the beach or rotting.
- **Brown kelps hold a lot of iodine.** Eat small amounts, especially if you are pregnant, a child, or have thyroid disease. Avoid hijiki: it holds arsenic.
- **A few seaweeds are harmful.** Acid kelp (Desmarestia) contains acid: spit out any piece that tastes very sour or burns. In tropical seas, some red seaweeds have killed people. There, eat only the kinds local people eat.
- **Slimy mats or hair-like strands** are often blue-green algae, not seaweed. They can burn skin and poison. Do not touch or eat them.
- Rinse in fresh water. Eat it cooked or dried. Dried seaweed keeps for months.
- Rinsed seaweed is a good fertilizer and mulch for gardens.

### Crabs

- Look under rocks and weed at low tide, or with a light at night. Bait a basket or bottle trap.
- Cook crabs alive or just killed, until the meat is opaque and hot all through. Throw away crabs that died before cooking.
- On tropical reefs, some small, colorful crabs carry the same nerve poisons as pufferfish. Cooking does not destroy them. Eat only the kinds local people eat.

### Seabirds and eggs

- Seabirds nest in colonies on cliffs and islands in spring and summer.
- **The law:** many countries protect seabirds and their eggs. Take them only in a true emergency.
- **Keep the colony alive.** Many seabirds lay only 1 egg a year. Take a few eggs from different nests, early in the laying season, when birds may lay again. Never take from rare birds. Leave quickly: frightened parents lose eggs and chicks to cold and gulls.
- Put eggs in water. Fresh eggs sink. Throw away eggs that float. Cook eggs until firm.
- **Cliffs kill egg collectors.** Edges break away, and droppings are slippery. Never collect alone.

### Driftwood and fuel

- Collect wood above the high-tide line, best after storms. Dry it for days: wet, salty wood is hard to light and smoky.
- **Never burn painted, tarred or treated wood** (often green-tinted), plastic or rope. The smoke is poisonous.
- Large logs make frames for shelters. Salt air rusts metal and rots rope fast: keep tools dry and oiled.

## Making salt

Seawater holds about **35 g of salt per liter** (about 2 level tablespoons per quart). You need salt to preserve fish and to replace what you sweat.

1. **Collect clean seawater,** away from river mouths, harbors, sewage and red tide. Let it settle, then pour it through a cloth.
2. **Let the sun do most of the work.** Pour it 2-5 cm (1-2 in) deep into shallow pans: trays, a plastic sheet in a shallow pit, or a clay-lined pan dug in the ground. Top up with seawater as it shrinks. This takes days to weeks.
3. **Harvest** the crystals when a thick layer forms, or finish the strong brine by boiling it in a pot.
4. **Stop before it is completely dry.** Pour off the last liquid. It holds bitter salts that taste bad and cause diarrhea.
5. **Dry the salt** in the sun. Store it covered and dry.

Boiling plain seawater wastes fuel. Boiling away 1 liter of water takes about 7 times the fuel needed just to bring it to a boil. Use the sun first.

## Fresh water by the sea

- **Rain** is the best source. Catch it from every roof and sheet.
- **Streams and rivers:** at high tide, salt water pushes up river mouths. Collect upstream, beyond the reach of the tide, or at low tide. Taste first.
- **Springs and seeps** run at the foot of cliffs and on some beaches at low tide.
- **Dig behind the dunes.** Fresh rainwater floats on top of salt water in the sand. Dig in a hollow behind the first dune, well above high tide, where plants grow. Stop as soon as water seeps in, and scoop gently from the top. Digging deeper or taking water fast pulls salt water up. Spit out water that tastes salty.
- **Green coconuts** hold safe liquid in the tropics. See [Finding Water](../water/find-water.md).
- **Distillation** makes seawater safe, but gives little water for a lot of fuel. A solar still gives at most 0.5-1 liter (2-4 cups) a day. Do not depend on it.
- Treat all fresh water. See [Making Water Safe to Drink](../water/purify-water.md).

## Tides

- Most coasts have **2 high and 2 low tides a day,** about **12 hours 25 minutes** apart. Each high tide comes about **50 minutes later** than the day before. Some coasts have 1 high and 1 low a day, or unequal tides. Watch for a few days.
- **Spring tides,** the highest highs and lowest lows, come around full moon and new moon. **Neap tides,** with the smallest change, come about a week later. Spring low tides are the best time to gather food.
- **Read the beach.** A line of fresh wet seaweed and rubbish (the strand line) marks the last high tide. Higher lines of dry seaweed and driftwood mark spring tides and storms. Barnacles and seaweed on rocks show how high the water reaches.
- **Keep a tide record.** Each day, write down the time of high tide. After a few days you can predict the next ones.

> [!WARNING]
> **The tide can cut you off.** On wide, flat beaches and mudflats it comes in fast and fills the channels behind you first. Never cross to an island, sandbar or rock, or walk below cliffs, unless you can be back well before high tide. Soft mud can trap your legs.
>
> If you are cut off, climb above the highest strand line and barnacles, and wait. Do not try to swim.

## Where to live

- **Above the highest storm line,** not just the high tide: the highest line of old driftwood and dead seaweed. Storms reach far higher than tides.
- **Behind the dunes or among trees,** out of wind and salt spray. Do not dig into or flatten dunes, or cut the plants on them. Dunes protect you from storms.
- **Not under cliffs** (falling rock), **not near cliff edges** (they break away), **not under coconut palms** (falling nuts).
- **Not on a river mouth or low spit of sand.** Storms flood them from both the sea and the river.
- **In tsunami areas,** sleep and keep stores on high ground: at least 30 m (100 ft) above sea level, or 3 km (2 miles) inland, if you can. Work on the shore by day. Walk your route to high ground and time it.

## Boats and rafts

- A raft is slow, hard to steer and easily carried out to sea by wind and current. Use any boat or raft only in sheltered water, close to shore, in calm weather.
- **Never go out when the wind blows from the land toward the sea.** It pushes you out faster than you can paddle back.
- Wear anything that floats. Never go out alone. Tell someone when you will be back.

## Dangers

### Storms and storm surge

Storm surge is sea water pushed onto land by a storm. Surge and flooding, not wind, cause most deaths in hurricanes and cyclones. Surge can rise 6 m (20 ft) or more, and it travels far inland up rivers. Low, flat coasts get the worst surge. Leave low ground before a big storm arrives. See [Hurricanes, Cyclones and Tornadoes](../disasters/storms.md).

### Rip currents, waves and cliffs

- **Rip currents** pull you out to sea, not under. Do not swim against one. Float, then swim parallel to the beach. Look for a gap in the breaking waves or a dark channel of water. See [Water and Ice Rescue](../navigation/water-rescue.md).
- **Never turn your back on the sea** on rocks or steep beaches. Single large waves sweep people off.
- Stay back from cliff edges. They overhang and break away.

### Stinging and biting sea animals

- **Jellyfish:** for box jellyfish (tropical Indo-Pacific and northern Australia), pour vinegar on the sting. See [Bites, Stings and Venom](../medical/bites-stings.md).
- **Stingrays:** shuffle your feet when wading on sand, so they swim away. Hot water eases the pain of a sting.
- **Sea urchins and stonefish:** wear shoes in the water.
- **Sea snakes** rarely bite, but their venom can kill. Leave them alone, even ones caught in nets or lying on the beach.
- **Cone snails:** never pick up cone-shaped shells in tropical seas. Their sting has killed people.

### Sun, glare and salt sores

- Water and sand reflect sunlight. You burn faster, and your eyes can burn too. Cover your skin, wear a wide hat and sunglasses, and stay in shade at midday.
- **Salt water sores** come from skin that stays wet with seawater for days. Rinse with fresh water and let the skin dry. Do not open or squeeze the sores. Put on antiseptic if you have it, and keep them clean and dry.
