> [!CAUTION]
> **Quicklime burns skin and can blind you.** It boils violently with water and spits hot, caustic paste. Wear eye protection, cover your skin, add lime to water slowly (never water onto lime), and keep children away. Lime in the eye: flush with clean water for **at least 30 minutes** and get help. See [Eye Injuries](../medical/eye-injuries.md).
>
> **Lime kilns and charcoal pits make carbon monoxide.** Never work them in a closed space or bring hot charcoal indoors. Never walk on a burning charcoal pit: you can fall into the fire. See [Carbon Monoxide and Smoke: The Silent Killers](../fire/carbon-monoxide.md).
>
> **Hot pine pitch catches fire and sticks to skin,** causing deep burns. Melt it beside the fire, never over open flame.

## Quick steps

1. **Clay:** mix sticky subsoil with sand, shape even walls, dry 1-2 weeks, fire slowly.
2. **Lime:** burn limestone or shells at about 900°C (1650°F) for many hours. Wearing eye protection, drop cooled lumps into 3 times their volume of water.
3. **Charcoal:** burn dry wood under soil with very little air, far from homes.
4. **Salt:** sun-dry clean seawater in shallow pans, then boil the brine. 1 liter (1 quart) gives about 35 g (1 oz).
5. **Cord:** twist each strand one way and wrap the strands together the other way.

## Clay and pots

### Find and prepare clay

- Look in riverbanks, lake beds, gullies and road cuts, below the topsoil. Clay is sticky when wet and hard when dry.
- **Coil test:** roll a piece as thick as a pencil and bend it around your finger. If it bends without cracking, it is good.
- **Clean it:** soak dry clay into thin mud, pour it through a cloth, let it settle, pour off the water, and dry it until it stops sticking to hands.
- **Add temper:** mix in clean sand or crushed old pottery until it feels slightly gritty, to stop cracking. Cooking pots need more.
- **Remove all bits of limestone and shell.** The fire turns them into quicklime, which later swells and breaks the pot.

### Shape, dry and fire

1. **Shape** by pinching a ball or building up rolls (coils) of clay. Keep walls even, about as thick as your little finger.
2. **Polish** the half-dry pot with a smooth stone to make it less porous.
3. **Dry slowly** in shade for 1-2 weeks, until it no longer feels cool against your cheek. A wet pot bursts in the fire.
4. **Warm it** near the fire for 1-2 hours, turning it, then at the edge of the coals.
5. **Fire:** set pots upside down on dry wood, bark or dung, pile more fuel over them, light it and keep feeding it. Open fires reach about **600-900°C (1100-1650°F).** Aim for the pots to glow red for at least an hour.
6. **Cool slowly** in the ashes until cold.
7. **Check:** soak a broken piece in water overnight. If it softens, it was not fired hot enough.

> [!NOTE]
> Expect to lose many pots, perhaps half, on your first firings. A firing pit out of the wind, heated slowly, breaks far fewer.

**Cooking pots** need thin, even walls and plenty of temper. Heat them slowly. Never put a cold pot on hot coals or let it boil dry.

## Lime: mortar, plaster, whitewash and maize

### Burn limestone or shells

1. **Find stone that fizzes** under a drop of vinegar or lemon juice: limestone, chalk, marble, sea shells or dead coral. Break stone into pieces no bigger than a fist.
2. **Build a kiln:** a pit or ring of stones about 1 m (3 ft) across, with an air channel at the bottom on the windward side. Load it in layers of dry wood and stone, ending with thick wood.
3. **Burn it hard for at least a day.** The stone must reach about **900°C (1650°F)** right through. A cooking fire is not hot enough.
4. **Let it cool completely,** for 1-2 days.
5. **Test the lumps:** burnt lime is chalky and much lighter than stone. Dropped in water, it hisses, gets hot and crumbles. Throw out hard, unburnt cores.

Store quicklime dry and closed, away from wood and straw. Wet quicklime can get hot enough to start a fire.

### Slake it into lime putty

1. **Dress for it:** goggles or tight glasses, a cloth over nose and mouth, gloves, long sleeves and closed shoes. Work outside, with children and animals far away and clean water ready.
2. **Put the water in a metal container** or stone-lined pit first: about **3 parts water to 1 part quicklime** by volume. Heat melts plastic.
3. **Add the quicklime a little at a time** with a shovel, and step back. It boils, steams and spits.
4. **Stir with a long-handled hoe** until no lumps remain. Keep the lime under water.
5. **When the bubbling stops,** cover it. Later, pour it through a fine sieve.
6. **Store it under a layer of water,** covered. Age it at least **3 months** before plastering, or leftover bits swell and pop out of the wall.

> [!WARNING]
> **Never pour water onto quicklime.** It flashes into steam and throws hot lime into your face. On skin, brush off dry powder first, then wash with lots of water. See [Burns](../medical/burns.md).

### Uses of lime

| Use | Mix | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mortar | 1 part putty to 3 parts sharp sand | Hardens slowly over weeks; protect from frost and rain |
| Plaster | 1 part putty to 2-3 parts sand, plus hair or chopped fiber | Dampen the wall; coats about 1 cm (0.5 in); mist with water for days |
| Whitewash (limewash) | Putty thinned with water to thin milk, plus a pinch of salt | Dampen the wall; several thin coats; mildly antibacterial, but it does not replace cleaning |

**Lime-cooked maize (nixtamal)** frees the niacin in maize, which helps prevent pellagra in people who live mostly on maize (see [Calorie Crops That Feed a Family for a Year](../farming/staple-crops.md)). Use slaked lime made from clean stone and clean wood. **Never put quicklime in food.**

1. Mix 10 g of slaked lime into each liter of water (1%), about 1 level tablespoon of dry powder.
2. Use 2 parts of this lime water to 1 part dry maize.
3. Heat to just below boiling for 30-60 minutes, until the skins slip off the kernels.
4. Soak overnight, rinse 2-3 times in clean water, and grind.

## Charcoal

1. Dig a pit about 1 m (3 ft) wide and deep, far from homes and downwind of them.
2. Light small dry wood in the bottom, then fill the pit closely with dry hardwood.
3. When it burns well, cover it with green leaves, then about 20 cm (8 in) of soil. Leave a few small air holes at the bottom edge.
4. Fill gaps where soil sinks, so flames never break through. When the smoke turns thin and pale blue, close all holes.
5. Wait until the soil is cold, usually 1-2 days. Wet any glowing pieces.

Expect at best 1 kg of charcoal from 4-5 kg of dry wood. It can relight hours later: store it outside.

## Salt

- **Seawater** holds about 35 g of salt per liter (about 2 tablespoons per quart). Collect it away from river mouths, harbors, sewage and oil.
- **Dry it in the sun first** in shallow pans, clay-lined pits or plastic sheets, adding more as it shrinks.
- **Boil the strong brine.** Scoop out crystals as they form and drain them.
- **Throw away the last bitter liquid.** Its magnesium salts cause diarrhea.
- **Salt springs and dry lake crusts** also give salt. Avoid bitter crusts and crusts near mines.
- Boiling seawater from the start burns a lot of firewood: 10 liters (2.5 gallons) give only about 300 g (10 oz) of salt. See [Living by the Sea](../land/coast-living.md).

## Plant fiber and cordage

| Plant | Part | How | Care |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stinging nettle | Stem skin | Crush stalks; peel fiber off the woody core | Gloves for green stems |
| Dogbane, milkweed, fireweed | Stem skin | Crush and peel dry autumn stalks | Dogbane and milkweed are poisonous to eat |
| Linden (basswood), willow, elm, cedar | Inner bark | Peel from felled trees; soak linden bark for weeks | Stripping bark around a living tree kills it |
| Yucca, agave, sisal | Leaf fiber | Pound leaves, scrape away pulp | Agave juice burns skin: wear gloves |
| Coconut and other palms | Husk fiber, leaf strips | Soak, pound and comb husk | Leaves rot when wet |

Good fiber does not snap when twisted hard. Never use poison ivy, poison oak or other plants that cause rashes.

### Reverse-wrap twisting

1. **Fold a long bundle** of fiber a little off the middle, so one strand is longer, and pinch the fold.
2. **Twist the far strand** tightly away from you.
3. **Wrap it toward you** over the other strand. It is now the near strand.
4. **Repeat** with the new far strand, moving your pinch along as the cord grows.
5. **Add fiber** when a strand is about 5 cm (2 in) from its end: lay new fiber along it and twist it in. Knot the end.

```
 REVERSE WRAP: 2-STRAND CORD

 fold      finished cord      pinch
  |                             |
  v                             v   ,====== A (far)
  (/////////////////////////////<
                                    '====== B (near)
```

If twist and wrap go the same way, the cord unravels. For rope, twist 3 cords together the same way. See [Essential Knots](../skills/knots.md).

## Pine pitch glue

1. **Collect hard resin** from wounds on pine, spruce or fir trees.
2. **Melt it gently** in a tin or shell at the edge of the coals, and skim out dirt. If it catches fire, smother it with a lid or soil.
3. **Stir in finely powdered charcoal,** up to about the same volume as the resin. More charcoal makes harder, more brittle glue.
4. **Use it warm** to fix blades in handles and seal seams. Store it wound on a stick.

Hot pitch on skin: cool it under clean water for 20 minutes. Do not peel it off.

## Hides and leather

- **Rawhide:** scrape off all flesh and fat, stretch the skin on a frame, and dry it. Wet strips shrink tight as they dry, making strong lashings.
- **Brain tanning (soft buckskin):** scrape off flesh, fat, hair and the thin outer skin. Soak the hide in a warm mash of brain and water, or egg yolks, and wring it out. Stretch and rub it constantly while it dries, or it dries stiff. Smoke both sides so it stays soft after getting wet. A deer hide takes about **10-20 hours** of hard work over 2-3 days.
- **Bark tanning:** soak the hide in strong oak bark tea, or another bark rich in tannin, for **weeks to months.**

> [!WARNING]
> Never use the hide of an animal found dead or that looked sick: anthrax and other diseases spread through skins. Wear gloves. Where deer and elk have chronic wasting disease, do not handle their brains: use egg yolks.

To get hides, see [Trapping and Preparing Small Game](../food/trapping.md).

## Containers

- **Hard-shell gourds (calabash, bottle gourd):** leave them on the vine until the stem dries, then dry them in airy shade until the seeds rattle, often for months. Cut an opening and scrape out the inside.
- **Never eat or drink gourd flesh or juice that tastes bitter.** It causes severe vomiting, bloody diarrhea and shock, even when cooked.
- **Bark:** birch bark peels easily in late spring and early summer. Fold it into boxes, sew the edges with roots or cord, and seal seams with pitch.
- **Bamboo:** cut just below a joint (node) for a cup or bottle with a closed bottom. To cook in green bamboo, always leave one end open: **a closed section bursts violently** when heated.
- To boil water in bark or wood, drop in fire-hot stones. Never heat river stones or wet stones: they can burst. See [Making and Keeping a Fire](../fire/make-fire.md).
