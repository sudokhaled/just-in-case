> [!CAUTION]
> **Never camp, sleep or build in a dry riverbed (wadi, arroyo, wash) or a narrow canyon.** A storm you cannot see can send a flash flood through in minutes, under a clear sky.
>
> **Do not ration water while you are thirsty, and do not work in the midday sun.** Save sweat, not water. Confusion or collapse in the heat is heat stroke: cool the person at once. See [Heat Exhaustion and Heat Stroke](../medical/heat-illness.md).
>
> **Keep your clothes on.** Loose clothes that cover your skin and head save water and stop sunburn.

## Quick steps

1. **Settle near reliable water,** on raised ground, never in a riverbed or on low ground beside it.
2. **Work at dawn, dusk and night.** Rest in deep shade from late morning to late afternoon.
3. **Drink enough to keep your urine pale.** Treat all water you find.
4. **Protect your water:** cover it, keep it in shade, keep animals and toilets away from the source.
5. **Make double shade first,** then build thick earth or stone walls with small openings.
6. **Shake out shoes, clothes and bedding.** Scorpions and snakes hide in them.
7. **Keep warm clothes and bedding.** Desert nights can freeze.

## Water is everything

### How much you need

| Situation | Drinking water |
|---|---|
| Resting in shade in hot weather | Plan at least 4-6 liters (1-1.5 gallons) per person per day |
| Working or walking below 38°C (100°F) | About 0.5 liter (1 pint) every hour |
| Working or walking above 38°C (100°F) | About 1 liter (1 quart) every hour |
| Hard work in full sun at 43°C (110°F) | About 19 liters (5 gallons) a day |

- The last line is more than a body can safely take in: never drink more than about 1.4 liters (1.5 quarts) in an hour. So never do hard work in the midday sun. See [Surviving Extreme Heat](../shelter/stay-cool.md).
- Cooking and washing need more: the humanitarian minimum for all uses is about 15 liters (4 gallons) per person per day. Keep water for handwashing.
- Eat salty food when you sweat for hours.
- People used to the heat sweat **more**, not less. Your body cannot learn to need less water.

### Where water is found

| Source | Where to look | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Oases and springs | Palm groves, reeds, green patches in low basins | Most reliable. Usually owned |
| Wells | Where paths and animal tracks meet | Keep covered: children fall in |
| Qanat, falaj, foggara | A line of mounds with shafts across a plain | Old tunnels bringing groundwater |
| Dry riverbeds | Outside of bends, lowest points, near green plants | Dig and wait. See [Finding Water](../water/find-water.md) |
| Rock pools (tinaja, gnamma, guelta) | Shaded hollows, foot of cliffs, below dry waterfalls | Hold rain for weeks. Often dirty |
| Rain | Short, violent storms | Catch it from sheets, roofs, rock |

- **Qanats** (Iran), **falaj** (Oman) and **foggara** (North Africa) feed whole towns. Take water only where the channel comes into the open. **Never climb down a shaft:** shafts are deep, collapse and can hold bad air.
- **Water belongs to someone.** Oases and wells have owners and old rules. In Oman, each falaj is shared out by time. Ask, and follow the rules. Fights over water kill.
- **Rock pools** keep wild animals alive. Take water out with a container. Do not bathe in them.
- **Digging:** Aboriginal Australians dig soaks in sandy creek beds. San people of the Kalahari suck water up through a hollow reed pushed into damp sand.
- **Treat all of it.** See [Making Water Safe to Drink](../water/purify-water.md). Desert sun makes the sunlight method (SODIS) work well.

### Dew and fog: honest yields

- **Dew** forms on clear, calm nights. Lay a plastic or metal sheet on dry grass or cloth, raised off the ground and tilted, with a container at the low edge. Collect before sunrise. On the best nights a good collector gives about 0.3-0.6 liter per square meter (1-2 cups per square yard). Most nights give less than 0.1 liter (half a cup), or nothing.
- **Fog** comes only to some coastal deserts and hills: the Atacama of Chile and Peru, the Namib, south-west Morocco, the mountains of southern Oman. Stretch a fine net or shade cloth upright across the wind on a ridge, with a gutter below into a covered container. Good sites give 3-10 liters per square meter of net per day, averaged over the year. Clear days give nothing.
- **Do not rely on a solar still.** It makes very little water and costs sweat to dig.

### Storing and protecting water

- Keep containers closed, in shade or buried in cool ground. Pour water out. Never dip hands or cups in.
- Porous clay pots and canvas or leather bags cool water as their damp outside dries. Hang them in shade and breeze.
- Hide sealed water along your routes, as San people do with buried ostrich eggshells. Mark each place.
- Fence the source against animals. Put toilets at least 30 m (100 ft) from it, and downhill.

## Working with the day

| Time | Do |
|---|---|
| Before dawn to mid-morning | Heavy work, gardening, collecting dew, travel |
| Late morning to late afternoon | Rest in deep shade. Light work in shade only |
| Evening and night | Travel, building, cooking, fetching water |

- **Clothes:** loose, light-colored, long sleeves and trousers. Wrap a long cloth around head and neck, like the Tuareg veil or the Arab head cloth. Pull it over mouth and nose in wind and dust.
- **Feet:** wear closed shoes. When the air is 43°C (110°F), sand can reach 60°C (140°F).
- **New arrivals** need 1-2 weeks to get used to the heat. Start with short work periods.
- **Eyes:** glare from sand can burn the eyes (sun blindness). Wear sunglasses, or tie a strip of card or leather with 2 slits about 3 mm (1/8 in) high across your eyes.

## Shelter that stays cool

### Shade first

- **Double shade:** stretch one sheet over another with an air gap of about 30-45 cm (12-18 in). A light or shiny top layer reflects the sun. Shade can be 11-17°C (20-30°F) cooler than the open desert.
- **Get off the hot ground.** Sit and sleep on a raised bed, mats or palm fronds, or scrape away the hot top layer of sand.

### Thick walls and dug-down homes

- **Earth and stone walls** 30-60 cm (1-2 ft) thick soak up the day's heat and pass it through slowly. Rooms stay cooler by day and warmer at night. Use mud brick (adobe), rammed earth or stone. See [Building a Lasting Home From Natural Materials](../land/build-home.md).
- **Few, small, shaded windows.** Whitewash outside walls and roofs. **Close up by day, open up at night.**
- **Dig down.** Ground a little below the surface stays much cooler by day. People live underground at Matmata in Tunisia and Coober Pedy in Australia. Dig only in firm ground on a rise, support the roof, and never dig where floods run.

### Courtyards and windcatchers

- **A courtyard** is a small open space with rooms around it. High walls shade it most of the day, and cool night air settles in it.
- **A windcatcher** is a tower whose top opening faces the wind: the badgir of Iran, the barjeel of the Gulf, the malqaf of Egypt. Wind high up is stronger and cleaner. It is pushed down into the rooms, and warm air leaves through high openings on the far side. The simplest version is a tall opening facing the wind and a high opening in the opposite wall.

### Tents

The black goat-hair tent of Bedouin and other nomads gives deep shade while air passes through the loose weave. In rain the hair swells and closes the gaps. Sides roll up in heat and drop down against wind, sand and cold. In the cold Gobi, herders use a felt-walled tent (ger).

## Food

- **Dates** are the oasis staple. Dried dates keep for months and give about 280 kcal per 100 g (3.5 oz). Palm fronds make roofs, mats and fences.
- **Animals:** goats, sheep and camels turn desert plants into milk and meat. Lizards, rodents and birds can be trapped. Camp away from a waterhole, or animals stop coming to drink.
- **Insects:** locusts and grasshoppers are eaten in many deserts. Remove legs and wings and cook well. Do not eat locusts from areas sprayed with insecticide.

### Growing food with little water

- **Plant in layers,** as oases do: date palms on top, fruit trees such as pomegranate, fig and olive below, vegetables and grain in their shade. See [Fruit Trees and Perennial Food Plants](../farming/perennial-food.md).
- **Sink the beds, do not raise them.** Dig beds a little below ground level with low earth walls around each, like the "waffle gardens" of the Zuni of New Mexico. Water collects at the roots and the walls block wind. Sahel farmers dig a small manure-filled pit at each plant (zai).
- **Mulch thickly.** Water at the roots, at dawn or dusk. A buried unglazed clay pot, or a bottle with small holes, drips water slowly to the roots. See [Watering Crops With Little Water](../farming/watering-crops.md).
- **Grow heat-hardy crops:** sorghum, pearl millet, cowpeas, tepary beans, melons, okra, prickly pear (remove all spines), dates and figs.
- **Use floods safely.** In Yemen, Sudan and Pakistan, low earth walls spread wadi flood water onto fields beside the channel, never in it.

## Fire and fuel

- Burn dead brush, dead shrub roots, palm fronds and stems, and dry dung of camels, cattle or goats. Floods leave driftwood in dry riverbeds.
- **Never cut living trees and shrubs** near an oasis or garden. Their roots hold the sand. Without them, sand buries gardens and wells.
- Use small fires, a pot lid and a stove. A solar cooker works very well in desert sun.
- Turn wood over with a stick before you pick it up. Scorpions hide under it.

## Dangers

### Flash floods

- Dark clouds or lightning upstream, a roar, or muddy water arriving mean a flood is coming.
- Build above the lines of branches and mud left high on the banks.
- **If water comes, climb straight up the side.** Do not run along the channel. See [Floods](../disasters/flood.md).

### Sandstorms and dust storms

Winds can reach 112-128 km/h (70-80 mph). Warning signs: a brown wall of dust, sudden strong wind, a yellow sky.

1. Get into a shelter, or behind a rock, wall or vehicle. Close it up.
2. Cover mouth and nose with cloth, wet if you can. Cover your eyes.
3. In the open, first **mark your direction of travel** with a stick or a line of stones.
4. Sit or lie with your back to the wind, head covered. Stay together and wait. Tracks vanish, so do not walk on.

### Cold nights

Desert air can be 55°C (131°F) by day and 10°C (50°F) at night. Winter nights can freeze, and the Gobi can fall to about -40°C (-40°F). Keep warm clothes, a hat and blankets, and sleep off the cold ground.

### Scorpions, snakes and spiders

Shake out shoes, clothes and bedding. Carry a light at night and sleep off the ground. Do not put hands under rocks or into holes. Give snakes a wide path. See [Bites, Stings and Venom](../medical/bites-stings.md).

### Getting lost: mirages and distance

- Empty desert makes most people guess distances about **3 times too short.** A hill that looks 1 km (half a mile) away may be 3 km (2 miles) away.
- Shimmering "lakes" on hot flat ground are mirages: hot air bending light. There are fewer at dawn and dusk.
- Mark your route, and tell others where you are going.

## Ask the people who live there

People have lived in every desert for thousands of years. Ask them where water is, which plants are safe and where floods run, and follow their rules for wells.

## Common mistakes and myths

- **"Save water for later."** People have died with water in their bottles. Drink it, and save sweat.
- **"A dry riverbed makes a good flat campsite."** It is the most dangerous place in the desert.
- **"Cactus is full of water."** Most cactus juice causes vomiting.
