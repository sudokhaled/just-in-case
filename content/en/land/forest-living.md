> [!CAUTION]
> **Look up before you camp, cut wood or rest.** Dead trees and dead branches ("widowmakers") fall without warning, most often in wind, wet snow or ice. Camp farther from any dead tree than it is tall.
>
> **In bear country, never keep food, garbage or cooking smells where you sleep.** A bear that gets food once comes back. Store food high in a tree or in a sealed container, 50 to 100 m (55 to 110 yards) downwind of your bed.
>
> **Cut winter firewood months ahead.** Fresh wood burns badly. Without dry wood, winter cold can kill.

## Quick steps

1. **Camp** part way up a slope, away from dead trees, river banks and valley bottoms.
2. **Take water** upstream of camp and treat all of it.
3. **Build a first shelter** on day 1, then improve it into a lasting home.
4. **Start winter wood now:** burn dead standing wood; cut and split fresh wood to dry for next winter.
5. **Cook and store food** far from your bed, out of reach of bears.
6. **Gather food daily:** fish, small game, and plants you can name with certainty.
7. **Check for ticks** every evening.
8. **Mark trails** so you can always find camp.

## Water

Streams, springs, lakes, rain and snow are common. **Treat all of it,** even clear spring water: beavers and other animals spread giardia and other germs. See [Making Water Safe to Drink](../water/purify-water.md).

- A spring is usually the cleanest source. Line a small pool below it with stones.
- Melting snow burns a lot of wood. In winter, keep a hole open in the ice near the shore instead.

## Firewood: your biggest job

**Dead standing trees and dead branches still on trees are the driest wood.** Wood lying on the ground soaks up water and rots. Dry wood is light, often cracked at the ends, and two pieces knocked together make a sharp "clack".

### How much for a winter

It depends on the cold, your shelter and your stove. Plan with this table, then watch how fast your pile shrinks in the first cold month.

| Situation | Stacked wood for one winter |
|---|---|
| Well-sealed house with a wood stove, cold temperate winter | About 7 to 18 m³ (2 to 5 cords) |
| Long northern winter, drafty cabin, or wet wood | Often twice as much or more |

- A **cord** is a tight stack 2.4 m long, 1.2 m high and 1.2 m deep (8 by 4 by 4 ft), about 3.6 m³ (128 cubic feet). A small, well-insulated cabin needs less.
- An open fire sends only about **10%** of its heat into the room. Even a simple closed stove made from a metal drum gives far more heat from the same wood.
- Cut extra for cooking. Running out in late winter is dangerous.

### Cut, split, dry, store

1. **Cut** lengths that fit your stove or fire, usually 30 to 60 cm (12 to 24 in).
2. **Split** every piece thicker than your wrist. Split wood dries much faster.
3. **Dry** fresh wood at least **6 months** if softwood (pine, spruce, fir) and **12 months** if hardwood (oak, maple, beech, birch). Wood you cut this year is for next winter.
4. **Stack** it off the ground on 2 poles or stones. Cover the **top only.** Open sides let sun and wind dry it.
5. Keep a few days' supply under a roof by the door for storms and deep snow.

## Food through the seasons

Most forest food comes in late summer and autumn, almost none in late winter. Dry or smoke food in autumn for winter.

| Season | Main foods |
|---|---|
| Spring | Fish runs, young greens such as nettle and dandelion |
| Summer | Berries, greens, fish, small game |
| Autumn | Nuts, late berries, fish, game: the time to store food |
| Winter | Stored food, trapping (snow shows animal trails), fishing through ice |

- **Learn each plant** in [Foraging Wild Plants Safely](../food/foraging.md) before you eat it. Deadly yew and nightshade berries grow in forests.
- **Nuts** are the richest wild food. Eat only nuts you can name. Acorns must be leached first. Horse chestnuts and buckeyes are poisonous: round, shiny, in a thick husk, with no scaly cup.
- **Do not eat wild mushrooms.** No test tells deadly ones from safe ones.
- **Fish and small game** give protein and fat. See [Catching Fish](../food/fishing.md) and [Trapping and Preparing Small Game](../food/trapping.md).
- **Inner bark is famine food only.** The soft pale layer under the bark of true pines (needles in bundles of 2, 3 or 5) can be boiled, or dried and ground into flour. It fills the stomach but gives little energy. Take it from branches or cut trees: bark stripped all around a trunk kills the tree. **Never use yew** (single flat needles): it is deadly.

## Materials from the forest

| Material | Where | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| Birch bark | Dead, fallen or cut birch | Tinder that lights when damp, containers, waterproof roof layer |
| Resin (pitch) | Wounds on pine, spruce, fir; resin-soaked wood in old pine stumps | Fire starter, glue (melted with fine charcoal), waterproofing |
| Fiber | Inner bark of linden (basswood), elm, cedar; spruce roots | Cord, rope, lashing |
| Clay | Stream banks, under topsoil | Sealing walls, chimneys, ovens, pots |

## Making camp a home

### Choosing the site

- **Gentle, well-drained ground, part way up a slope.** Valley bottoms collect cold air, frost, fog and floods.
- **No dead trees that could reach you.** Check again after every storm.
- **Near water, but above flood marks** (mud lines and debris on trees) and at least 60 m (200 ft) from it.
- **In bear country,** at least 50 m (55 yards) from animal trails, berry patches and thick brush.
- **Winter sun:** a slope facing south in the northern half of the world, facing north in the southern half.
- **Living trees as a windbreak,** and dead wood within easy reach.

**Layout:** sleeping shelter upwind. Kitchen and food store 50 to 100 m (55 to 110 yards) downwind of it; cook, eat and wash dishes there only. Latrine downhill, at least 60 m (200 ft) from water. Water point upstream of everything.

### From first shelter to lasting home

| When | Goal |
|---|---|
| Day 1 | Tarp, lean-to or debris hut, with a thick bed of leaves or boughs |
| Weeks 1 to 2 | Raised bed, a log wall behind the fire to reflect heat, a ditch uphill to carry rain away |
| Months 1 to 3 | A small hut or cabin with a real roof and a safe chimney or stove |
| Before winter | Insulated walls and roof, tight door, full woodpile, stored food |

Keep it small: a small space is far easier to heat. Build roofs steep and clear heavy snow off them. See [Building a Lasting Home From Natural Materials](../land/build-home.md) and [Staying Warm in the Cold](../shelter/stay-warm.md). **Never burn a fire or stove in a closed shelter without a chimney.** Carbon monoxide kills in your sleep.

**Every day:** treat water, cut more wood than you burned, check traps and lines, store food before dark, and check for ticks.

## Forest dangers

### Falling trees

Widowmakers are dead branches caught in trees, dead or burned trees, broken tops, and trees leaning on other trees. The danger is greatest in strong wind, wet snow and ice: then move to a clearing. Never work under a tree leaning on another, and do not try to cut it down.

### Axe and saw injuries

- **Keep the axe sharp.** A dull axe glances off the wood into your leg.
- **Clear the swing area:** hold the axe at arm's length, turn a full circle, and remove every twig it touches. Keep everyone well out of reach.
- Split on a wide chopping block, so a miss hits wood, not your leg.
- Trim branches from the far side of a fallen trunk, with the trunk between you and the axe.
- A sapling bent under a fallen tree can spring out with great force. Cut it gently on the inside of the bend.
- Felling is the most dangerous job in the woods. Without training, fell only small trees, never in wind, never alone. Never stand directly behind a falling tree.

### Wildfire

Clear dry grass, leaves and dead branches around camp. Know 2 escape routes to a large clearing, lake, river or burned ground. If you smell smoke, leave early. Never light an open fire on a dry, windy day. See [Wildfires and House Fires](../disasters/wildfire.md).

### Getting lost

People get lost a few hundred meters from their own camp. Mark trails with cuts on trees or cloth strips, each mark visible from the last. Carry a knife, fire starter and whistle. See [What to Do When You Are Lost](../navigation/lost.md).

### Ticks

Ticks spread Lyme disease in North America, Europe and Asia, and tick-borne encephalitis in Europe and northern Asia. Tuck long trousers into socks and use repellent if you have it.

- **Check every evening:** under the arms, in and around the ears, the belly button, behind the knees, the hair, between the legs and around the waist. Check children and dogs too. Lyme disease usually needs the tick attached for 36 to 48 hours, so daily checks protect you.
- **Remove a tick** with fine tweezers: grasp it as close to the skin as possible and pull straight out with steady, even pressure. Do not twist. If mouthparts stay in, leave them. Wash the bite and your hands. Do not use heat, oil or nail polish.
- **For several weeks after a bite,** fever, a spreading rash or a red ring around the bite needs a health worker and antibiotics.

### Bears

**Storing food:**

1. Keep all food, garbage, fish guts, pots, soap and toothpaste at the kitchen. Never in the shelter.
2. Hang them in a bag from a strong live branch: the bottom of the bag at least **3.7 m (12 ft)** off the ground and at least **1.8 m (6 ft)** out from the trunk and from any other branch. Higher is better: some forests require 6 m (20 ft). Bear-proof containers or sealed metal drums are safer still.
3. Do not bury food scraps: bears dig them up. Burn them completely in a hot fire at the kitchen, far from where you sleep. Never leave half-burned food or greasy ash in the fire pit.

**If you meet a bear:** do not run: bears are faster. Pick up small children. Talk calmly in a low voice. Back away slowly, leaving the bear a way out. Do not climb a tree: both kinds climb. Never come between a mother and her cubs. Use bear spray on a charging bear if you have it.

**If a bear attacks:**

| Bear | What to do |
|---|---|
| Brown or grizzly bear, especially one you surprised or a mother with cubs | Leave your pack on. Lie on your stomach, hands behind your neck, legs spread. Stay still until it leaves. Fight back if the attack lasts more than about 2 minutes |
| Black bear | Do not play dead. Escape into a building if you can. If not, fight hard: hit its face and nose with rocks or sticks |
| Any bear that stalks you, or attacks at night or in your shelter | Fight back with everything you have. It sees you as food |

**Telling them apart:** brown and grizzly bears have a shoulder hump and short round ears. Black bears have no hump and taller ears. **Color does not tell you:** black bears can be brown or blond. Brown bears live in North America, Europe and Asia; American black bears only in North America.

### Cougars, wolves and moose

- **Cougars (mountain lions) and other big cats:** do not run. Face the animal, raise your arms to look big, and pick up small children without crouching. If attacked, fight back on your feet.
- **Wolves** rarely attack people. Do not run. Stand tall, shout and back away slowly. Fight back if attacked.
- **Moose** kick and trample, especially mothers with calves. If one charges, run and get behind a tree.
