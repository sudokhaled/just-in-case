> [!CAUTION]
> **Never put fresh feces, from people or animals, on growing food crops.** They carry the germs and worm eggs that cause diarrhea, typhoid, hepatitis and worms. Treat them first, dig them into the soil, and wait at least 4 months (120 days) before harvesting crops that touch the soil, 3 months (90 days) for others.
>
> **Never use dog or cat feces on food crops,** however old.
>
> **Wash your hands with soap** after handling manure, compost or urine. Wash all produce in safe water.

## Quick steps

1. **Test your soil:** squeeze a damp handful. Gritty and falling apart is sand. Sticky, making a long ribbon, is clay.
2. **Add organic matter every season:** mix 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of compost or aged manure into the top 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in).
3. **Cover bare soil** with 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) of mulch.
4. **Make compost:** 2 to 3 buckets of dry "browns" to 1 bucket of fresh "greens", as damp as a wrung-out sponge, in a heap at least 1 m (3 ft) wide and high.
5. **Feed crops** with aged manure, or urine mixed 1 part to 3 parts water, poured on the soil. Stop urine 1 month before harvest.
6. **Grow legumes** (beans, peas) in every bed in turn, and move each crop family to a new bed every year.

## Read your soil by hand

### Squeeze and ribbon test

1. Take a walnut-sized lump from 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) down. Wet it and knead it like soft putty.
2. Squeeze it. If it will not hold together, it is sand.
3. If it holds, push it between thumb and finger into a ribbon until it breaks.

| Result | Soil | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Falls apart, no ribbon | Sand | Dries fast, loses plant food |
| Ribbon under 2.5 cm (1 in) | Loam | Best for most crops |
| Ribbon 2.5-5 cm (1-2 in) | Clay loam | Holds water and food; hard to dig |
| Ribbon over 5 cm (2 in) | Clay | Drains slowly, sticky when wet, cracks when dry |

### Jar test

Fill a tall clear jar one quarter with dry, crushed soil and add water to three quarters. Shake hard for 10 to 15 minutes, then leave it still. Mark the layers: sand after **1 minute**, silt after **2 hours**, clay when the water clears (**1 to 3 days**). Loam is roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay.

### Drainage test

Dig a hole 30 cm (1 ft) deep. Fill it with water and let it drain. Fill it again at once, measure the drop in 15 minutes, and multiply by 4.

- **Less than 2.5 cm (1 in) per hour:** poor drainage. Use raised beds.
- **2.5 to 7.5 cm (1 to 3 in) per hour:** good.
- **More than 10 cm (4 in) per hour:** very fast, usually sand. Add organic matter and mulch.
- **Water seeps in from below:** high water table. Grow on raised beds or mounds.

## Fixing poor soil

**Organic matter fixes almost every soil.** It helps sand hold water and opens clay to air. Add it every season. Do not burn crop remains, except diseased plants.

- **Clay or wet ground:** raise beds 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in) above the paths (see [When and How to Plant](../farming/planting-guide.md)). **Do not add sand to clay:** it sets harder. Do not dig or walk on clay that squeezes into a sticky ball.
- **Sand or dry ground:** in dry climates, sink beds 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) below the paths so water collects at the roots. Mulch thickly.
- **Hard crust:** dig planting pits filled with manure (see [Watering Crops With Little Water](../farming/watering-crops.md)).
- **Alkaline soil:** pour vinegar on a spoonful of dry soil. If it fizzes, it holds lime: add no wood ash. Soils in rainy areas and pine forests are often acid.
- **White crust in dry areas** is salt. Where the soil drains, water deeply with fresh water to wash it down. Add no urine or ash there.

## Compost

### Hot compost: faster, kills many weed seeds and germs

1. **Build it in one go,** at least 1 m (3 ft) wide, long and high. Smaller heaps do not heat.
2. **Mix 2 to 3 parts browns to 1 part greens** by volume, chopped to 5 to 15 cm (2 to 6 in).
3. **Water as you build.** A squeezed handful should drip only a few drops.
4. **Check the heat** after 3 to 7 days: a stick left in the middle for 5 minutes should come out too hot to hold, about 55 to 65°C (130 to 150°F).
5. **Turn it** when it cools, every 3 to 7 days for the first month, outside to the middle. Add browns if it smells, water if dry.
6. **Let it cure** 1 to 2 months after it stops heating. Finished compost is dark, crumbly and smells of forest soil.

![Hot compost heap cut in half: 1 at least 1 m (3 ft) wide, long and high. 2 browns (dry leaves, straw, dry grass, chopped stalks): 2 to 3 parts by volume. 3 greens (scraps, fresh grass, weeds, manure): 1 part. 4 water as you build: a squeezed handful should drip only a few drops. 5 stick left in the hot middle (red) for 5 minutes: it should come out too hot to hold, about 55 to 65°C (130 to 150°F). 6 turn it when it cools, every 3 to 7 days for the first month, outside to the middle.](../../images/compost-pile.svg)

> [!NOTE]
> The US organic standard counts manure as composted only after 15 days at 55 to 77°C (131 to 170°F), turned at least 5 times. Home heaps rarely heat that evenly, so dig manure compost in before planting.

**Cold compost:** pile materials as they come, keep the heap damp and covered, and wait **6 to 12 months**. It does not kill weed seeds or germs, so keep seeding weeds and manure out.

| Greens (nitrogen) | Browns (carbon) | Never put in |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds | Dry leaves, straw, dry grass | Meat, fish, bones, fat, milk products: they bring rats and flies |
| Fresh grass, weeds before they seed | Chopped maize, sorghum and millet stalks | Dog, cat or human feces |
| Manure of plant-eating animals and poultry | Sawdust of untreated wood | Diseased plants, roots of spreading weeds |
| Bean leaves, diluted urine | Cardboard, plain paper, a little wood ash | Coal ash, treated wood, plastic |

## Animal manure

| Manure | How to use it |
|---|---|
| Cow, buffalo, camel, horse, donkey | Mild. Often full of weed seeds: hot-compost it if you can. |
| Goat, sheep, rabbit | Stronger, dry pellets. Easy to compost or dig in. |
| Chicken, duck, pigeon | Strongest: fresh, it burns seedlings. Compost or age it 2 to 6 months (sources vary). |
| Pig | Carries worms that infect people. Hot-compost it, for fruit trees or cooked crops only. |
| Dog, cat | **Never on food crops.** Carries roundworms and toxoplasma. |

The US organic standard requires raw manure to be dug into the soil:

- At least **4 months (120 days) before harvest** for crops whose eaten part touches the soil (lettuce, carrots, potatoes, strawberries).
- At least **3 months (90 days) before harvest** for other food crops (maize, staked tomatoes, climbing beans).

## Human urine

Urine from healthy people holds few germs and is mostly a nitrogen fertilizer. One adult makes about 1.5 liters (1.5 quarts) a day.

**WHO rules:**

- **For your own household's food,** urine can be used fresh. Many programs still store it 1 month first.
- **For food that others will eat,** store it closed for **at least 6 months at 20°C (68°F) or warmer.** Stored less, or colder, use it only on crops that will be cooked, or on fodder.
- **Stop at least 1 month before harvest.** For crops eaten raw, work it into the soil.
- **Keep it separate from feces.** Mixed, it needs the treatment for feces.
- **During a cholera or typhoid outbreak, do not use urine on crops.** Pour it into a latrine (see [Toilets and Sanitation](../hygiene/sanitation.md)).

**How to use it:**

1. Mix **1 part urine with 3 parts water,** the most common of mixes ranging from 1:1 to 1:15.
2. Pour it into a shallow furrow beside the row, never on leaves. Cover with soil or water it in.
3. Per growing season, use about **1.5 liters of undiluted urine per square meter** (1.5 quarts per 11 sq ft). Split it into 2 or 3 feeds: at planting, 2 to 4 weeks later, and once more for long crops.
4. Use it on hungry crops such as maize, cabbage and leafy greens, not on beans.
5. Store it in closed containers. Open, the nitrogen escapes as ammonia.

Keep urine out of wells, streams and ponds. On salty soil, too much adds salt.

## Human feces

**Never use fresh or partly rotted feces on food crops.** Use only feces kept dry and covered, after one of these WHO household treatments, adding no fresh feces during that time:

| Treatment | Time |
|---|---|
| Storage at an average 2-20°C (36-68°F) | 1.5 to 2 years |
| Storage at an average 20-35°C (68-95°F) | More than 1 year |
| Plenty of ash or lime (pH above 9), above 35°C (95°F) and dry | More than 6 months |
| Hot composting in turned or aerated heaps | 1 month at 55-60°C (130-140°F), then 2 to 4 months to mature |

Without pH test strips, use the storage times. Two pits, used in turn, give the waiting time. Dig the result in before planting, preferably for fruit trees or cooked crops, and wait at least 1 month before harvest.

## Wood ash

- Ash adds potassium (potash) and calcium, and raises soil pH faster than lime.
- **Use it only on acid soil** (no fizz with vinegar). Never on alkaline or salty soil.
- **No more than about 1 cup (250 mL) per square meter per year:** a 20-liter bucket per 100 m² (5 gallons per 1,000 sq ft). Some services advise a quarter of that.
- Spread it thin and mix it in, never together with fresh manure or urine: it drives off their nitrogen.
- Use only clean wood ash, never from coal, treated wood or rubbish. Wet ash burns skin.

## Legumes and green manure

Beans, peas, cowpeas, groundnuts (peanuts), lentils, chickpeas, pigeon peas and clovers host bacteria in root lumps (nodules) that take nitrogen from the air. Working nodules are pink or red inside.

- **Green manure** is a crop grown to feed the soil. Cut it as it starts to flower, then mulch with it or dig it in. Wait about 2 to 3 weeks before sowing into it.
- **Hot climates:** velvet bean (mucuna), lablab, cowpea, sunn hemp, jack bean, pigeon pea.
- **Cool seasons:** field beans (fava), peas, vetch, clovers.

> [!WARNING]
> **Do not eat seeds of velvet bean, jack bean, sunn hemp, vetch or bitter lupin** unless you know a proven local way to make them safe. They are poisonous. Boil lablab beans well.

## Crop rotation

Pests and diseases of one plant family build up in the soil. Move each family to a new bed yearly, returning only after **3 to 4 years**.

| Family | Crops |
|---|---|
| Legumes | Beans, peas, cowpeas, groundnuts, lentils, soybeans |
| Nightshade | Tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, chilies, eggplant (aubergine, brinjal) |
| Cabbage | Cabbage, kale, mustard, turnip, radish |
| Squash | Pumpkin, squash, cucumber, melon |
| Onion | Onion, garlic, leek |
| Grasses | Maize (corn), sorghum, millet, wheat, rice |

A simple order: **legumes, then a hungry crop** (maize, cabbage, squash or tomatoes), **then root crops, then legumes again.**

## Signs of nutrient shortage

| Sign | Likely shortage | What to add |
|---|---|---|
| Older (lower) leaves pale yellow all over | Nitrogen | Diluted urine, compost, manure |
| Older leaves dark green with purple or red | Phosphorus (or cold soil) | Compost, manure |
| Older leaves brown and dry at the edges | Potassium | Ash on acid soil, compost |
| Older leaves yellow between green veins | Magnesium | Compost, manure |
| Youngest leaves yellow between green veins | Iron, often from alkaline or waterlogged soil | Compost, drainage, no ash |
| Black, sunken spot at the flower end of tomatoes or peppers | Calcium, usually from uneven watering | Water evenly, mulch |

Wrong watering, cold, root damage, pests and disease look similar: check those first (see [Protecting Crops From Pests, Disease and Animals](../farming/crop-pests.md)).
