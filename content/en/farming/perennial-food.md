> [!CAUTION]
> **Cassava and chaya contain cyanide.** Never eat them raw. Boil chaya leaves for 20 minutes in an open pot, and pour off the water. For cassava, see [Calorie Crops That Feed a Family for a Year](../farming/staple-crops.md).
>
> **Never eat rhubarb leaves, moringa roots, or horse chestnuts** (a poisonous look-alike of sweet chestnuts: see [Foraging Wild Plants Safely](../food/foraging.md)). Do not drink raw katuk juice.
>
> **Trees take 2 to 10 years to feed you.** Plant them together with fast annual crops, never instead of them.

## Quick steps

1. **Choose plants that survive your coldest night and longest dry season.** Plants thriving nearby are the safest choice.
2. **Plant fast perennials** (papaya, banana, cassava, chaya, moringa, berries) for food in 1 to 2 years, and slow trees at the same time.
3. **Start plants cheaply** from cuttings, suckers and layers.
4. **Plant in a wide hole no deeper than the roots,** with no fertilizer in the hole.
5. **Water deeply** in dry spells for the first 2 to 3 years, and mulch.
6. **Guard young trees** from animals, and prune dead, sick and crossing branches.

## Choosing plants for your climate

- **Frost kills tropical plants** such as banana, papaya, coconut and moringa. Young mango trees die at -1 to -2°C (28 to 30°F).
- **Apples, pears, cherries and most plums need a cold winter** to flower. In the lowland tropics they bear little fruit.

Times count from planting; poor soil, drought and shade make them longer. Sources differ.

## Seeds, cuttings or suckers

**Seedlings of most fruit trees do not come true.** An apple seed carries a new mix of 2 parents, so its fruit is different, usually worse. The same is true of pears, plums, cherries, avocados and many mangoes. Good varieties are kept by grafting; seedlings make rootstocks.

**True or nearly true from seed:** papaya, moringa, coconut, many citrus, and "polyembryonic" mangoes (most Southeast Asian types, not most Indian types). Seedling nut trees give edible nuts of mixed quality.

**Plant within days:** mango, avocado and jackfruit seeds die if they dry out.

### Cuttings

**Hardwood cuttings** root easily for fig, grape, pomegranate, mulberry and currant:

1. In the dormant season, after the leaves fall, cut pencil-thick shoots of last year's growth.
2. Cut pieces about 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in) long. Cut flat just below a bud at the bottom and slanted above a bud at the top, so you know which end is up. **Upside-down cuttings do not root.**
3. Push them into moist soil or a pot, leaving only the top 5 cm (2 in) out.
4. Keep them damp, not wet, in light shade. Move them after one growing season.

**Big stem cuttings in the tropics,** planted at the start of the rains: cassava pieces 20 to 25 cm (8 to 10 in) long (see [Saving Seeds](../farming/save-seeds.md)); moringa branches about 1 m (3 ft) long and 2.5 cm (1 in) thick, buried one-third deep; chaya and katuk woody stem pieces about 30 cm (12 in) long.

### Suckers, offshoots and division

- **Banana, plantain:** dig suckers with narrow, sword-shaped leaves, not broad leaves.
- **Date palm:** take offshoots 3 to 5 years old with their own roots, weighing 10 to 25 kg (22 to 55 lb). Grow them in a shaded nursery bed for 1 to 2 years before planting out.
- **Breadfruit:** dig up shoots from its roots. Divide raspberries, rhubarb, asparagus and Jerusalem artichoke.

### Layering

Bend a low branch to the ground. Scrape the bark underneath, bury that part 10 cm (4 in) deep and pin it, tip upright. When it has roots, usually after one season, cut it free and move it. This works for grapes, currants, hazelnuts and figs.

**Air layering** (citrus, lychee, guava, fig):

1. Remove a ring of bark 2 to 3 cm (1 in) wide from a branch as thick as a finger.
2. Wrap the ring in a fist-sized ball of damp moss or soil, cover it with plastic, and tie both ends.
3. When roots show, often after 1 to 3 months, cut below the ball and plant it in shade.

### Grafting: the idea

A twig (scion) from a good tree is joined to a young seedling (rootstock) of the same kind, such as apple to apple or mango to mango. The thin green layer under the bark of both must touch and grow together. Fruit above the joint is the same as on the tree the twig came from.

**Simple cleft graft** (late winter in cold climates; warm weather in the tropics):

1. Cut the rootstock straight across where it is pencil- to thumb-thick, and split the top down the middle 3 to 4 cm (1.5 in).
2. Cut the base of a scion with 2 or 3 buds into a wedge about 3 cm (1 in) long.
3. Push the wedge into the split so the green layers line up on at least one side.
4. Wrap the joint tightly with plastic strips. Seal the scion's cut top with wax, clay or plastic.
5. Rub off shoots that grow below the joint.

Expect failures at first: graft several.

## Planting a tree

1. **Choose the spot:** full sun, soil that drains, and room for the grown tree. An unpruned mango needs 7 to 9 m (25 to 30 ft) from buildings. Plant at the start of the rains, or in early spring where winters are cold.
2. **Dig a hole** 2 to 3 times as wide as the roots, and no deeper. Loosen its sides.
3. **No fertilizer or fresh manure in the hole:** it kills young trees. Compost may be mixed into the dug soil, at most half and half.
4. **Set the tree at the depth it grew before.** Keep the graft joint 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) above the soil.
5. **Refill, firm gently,** make a ring of soil to hold water, and water at once.
6. **Mulch** 5 to 15 cm (2 to 6 in) deep, kept 20 to 30 cm (8 to 12 in) from the trunk.
7. **Stake only on windy sites.** Tie with cloth or natural string, never wire. Remove it after 1 year.
8. **Protect it** from animals: fence it, wrap the trunk in wire mesh, or ring it with thorny branches.
9. **Water** every 2 days for the first week. Then, in dry spells, give 10 to 20 L (3 to 5 gallons) each week for the first 2 to 3 years.

For saving water, see [Watering Crops With Little Water](../farming/watering-crops.md).

## Pruning

- Use a sharp, clean blade. Cut just outside the swollen ring where a branch joins the trunk, leaving no stub.
- **Any time:** cut out dead, diseased and broken branches, and burn or bury diseased wood.
- **Once a year** (late winter where winters are cold; after harvest in the tropics): remove crossing branches, shoots growing straight up through the middle, and shoots from below the graft.
- **Young trees:** keep 3 to 5 strong branches around the trunk, with an open center.
- Remove no more than about a quarter of the living branches in one year.
- Cut moringa, chaya and katuk back to 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft) so the leaves stay in reach.
- **Bananas:** keep 1 fruiting stem and 1 or 2 young suckers in each clump. Remove the others.
- Do not climb tall trees to prune: falls cause serious injuries.

## Food forests and agroforestry

**Agroforestry** means growing trees and crops together on the same land. A **food forest** copies a natural forest in layers: tall trees (nuts, breadfruit, coconut, mango), small trees (citrus, papaya, apple), shrubs (berries, chaya, cassava), vegetables, ground cover (sweet potato, pumpkin) and climbers (beans, yams, grapes).

**Nitrogen-fixing trees** such as gliricidia, leucaena and sesbania (tropics) or alder (cold places) feed the soil. Cut their branches often and lay them under your crops as mulch. Grow them for soil, not food: parts of some are poisonous.

A food forest takes many years to mature and gives mostly fruit, leaves and nuts. Grow staple crops in the sunny gaps while the trees are young. For feeding the soil, see [Soil, Compost and Natural Fertilizer](../farming/soil-compost.md). For animals and pests, see [Protecting Crops From Pests, Disease and Animals](../farming/crop-pests.md).
